Kpler Shows Hormuz Commodity Ship Transits Fell to 5 Saturday and 0 Sunday

The Strait of Hormuz did not close. It did something more annoying for everyone who buys fuel: it slowed into a weekend bottleneck after another cluster of tanker attacks.

The weekend traffic number is the development

Kpler data reported by Reuters showed only 5 commodity vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday, November 21, and none registered for Sunday, November 22. The prior weekend had 31 such transits. Before the war, more than 130 ships a day moved through the strait, according to the same report. That is the difference between a global energy artery and a very expensive waiting room.

The slowdown followed reported attacks on tankers and came as U.S.-Iran talks remained stalled. The Reuters report, carried by Gulf Times, said some ships may still pass with transponders off, so the count is not a perfect census. But it is still a hard market signal: ships, charterers and insurers are behaving as if the passage is dangerous, not merely inconvenient. Reuters reported the Kpler transit figures on November 23.

That matters because Hormuz handled about a fifth of the world’s crude oil and liquefied natural gas shipments before the war. Even when barrels keep moving, the system gets slower, less predictable and more expensive to operate. A tanker that waits, detours, sails dark, joins a convoy, pays higher insurance, or needs repair after a strike is not just a geopolitical headline. It is a cost moving toward refinery gates, diesel racks, farm fuel tanks and airline schedules.

The bottom line: Kpler tracked 5 commodity-vessel transits through Hormuz on Saturday and none on Sunday, down from 31 the prior weekend. That is not a full shutdown, but it is enough to keep oil, diesel, freight and insurance markets jumpy.

JMIC says 7 tankers have been struck since November 16

The shipping slowdown did not appear out of nowhere. The Joint Maritime Information Center’s Update 116, issued with information current to 1300 UTC on November 22, kept the regional maritime threat level at severe. It listed three Strait of Hormuz tanker attacks in the latest 72-hour period: one unnamed tanker struck on the port bow on November 20, the tanker TORKEL struck on the stern on November 21, and the tanker ARINA struck in the engine room on November 21.

JMIC said all three were outbound tankers hit by unknown projectiles 8 to 17 nautical miles from Khasab, Oman, within about 15 hours. The advisory also said 7 tankers had been struck in the Strait of Hormuz since November 16. The point is not that every strike sank a ship. The point is that the route is generating repeated repair, fire, crew-safety and environmental-risk decisions for operators who would normally prefer their cargoes to arrive without becoming case studies. JMIC’s November 22 advisory lists the incidents and the 7-tanker count.

That is why the weekend transit collapse is more useful than another grand statement about whether Hormuz is open or closed. Open is not binary in shipping. A channel can be technically open and commercially half-frozen. If enough owners decide the next passage is not worth the premium, the result looks a lot like reduced capacity.

Oil prices are still carrying the risk premium

Oil prices did not explode on Monday morning, which is worth saying because panic is a bad analyst and an even worse purchasing department. Reuters reported Brent at $92.67 a barrel and WTI at $83.57 by 0423 GMT on November 23, with both contracts little changed in Asian trade after dropping the previous week. That is not cheap oil. It is also not a market pricing a clean return to normal.

The important part is the floor, not the one-minute move. Persistent shipping and refinery attacks are keeping a risk premium in the barrel even when inventories or equity-market weakness push the other way. For ordinary people, that risk premium usually shows up unevenly: first in diesel, heating oil, jet fuel and freight-heavy goods, and only later, or less cleanly, in the gasoline price sign you actually notice.

This is also why the latest development should not be read as a guaranteed shortage. It is better understood as a narrowing of choices. If Hormuz runs normally, refiners, traders and shipowners have options. If Hormuz runs by exception, every barrel has to be scheduled around risk, escorts, insurance and timing. That is how a chokepoint becomes a billing department with water.

U.S. inventories help, but the cushion is uneven

The United States did get some near-term help from inventories. In the EIA report for the week ending November 13, commercial crude stocks rose by 3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million barrels. Gasoline stocks rose by 5.2 million barrels to 215.9 million. Distillate stocks, the bucket that includes diesel and heating oil, rose by 1.6 million barrels to 104.9 million.

That is useful. It is not magic. Distillate stocks were still 5.6% below the same week a year earlier, and low-sulfur distillate was 6.7% below last year. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve also fell by 3.2 million barrels on the week to 276.4 million barrels. In plain English: the U.S. had a better weekly stock report, but the diesel cushion is still thin and the emergency crude reserve is still being used as a shock absorber. The EIA’s Weekly Petroleum Status Report provides the latest U.S. stock data.

That is the split readers should keep in mind. A good inventory week can soften the blow from a bad shipping week. It does not erase it. This is the same inventory-versus-chokepoint tension covered recently in the earlier Notavello look at U.S. fuel builds and Mideast export losses, only now the transit count has moved from weak to nearly stalled for a weekend.

The consequence is not just oil; it is scheduling

The market consequence is easy to miss if you only watch the crude price. A delayed tanker can mean a refinery waits longer for feedstock. A repaired tanker can mean cargo availability shifts by days or weeks. A dark transit can complicate tracking and financing. A war-risk premium can change which cargoes are worth lifting at all. None of this requires every vessel to stop.

For agriculture, the diesel link is the obvious one. Diesel moves grain, runs some farm equipment, powers harvest logistics, and feeds into trucking and rail costs. Fertilizer also sits close to the energy system because ammonia and urea production depend heavily on natural gas, while international fertilizer trade depends on bulk shipping, port access and insurance. A Hormuz slowdown does not automatically mean fertilizer scarcity in Iowa or Nebraska next week. But it does remove slack from a system that was already being watched carefully.

For households, the cleaner translation is this: do not expect every headline about lower crude to flow straight through to cheaper necessities. If refined products, insurance, freight and regional supply routes remain stressed, the pump and grocery effects can lag, split by region, or show up in less obvious places. Energy markets love making the simple version wrong. It is practically a hobby.

What to watch next

The useful indicators are concrete, not theatrical. Watch whether tracked Hormuz transits rebound from the weekend collapse, whether JMIC and UKMTO advisories show more engine-room or hull strikes, whether crude and product tankers keep sailing dark, and whether diesel inventories keep building into winter demand. Also watch whether Bab el-Mandeb and Red Sea routes stay available enough to relieve pressure, because one chokepoint problem is manageable and two are a logistics migraine.

The current read is therefore cautious, not apocalyptic. Hormuz has not been shut. Oil is still moving. U.S. inventories just improved. But Kpler’s weekend count and JMIC’s 7-tanker strike tally show why the market is not relaxing. A route that used to move routine cargoes now needs a risk committee, a naval update and a very patient insurer. That is not normal commerce. That is commerce wearing a helmet.
