Seventy Six Cents Is The Grain Freight Bill

Diesel eased a few cents. Grain freight did not get the memo, because the surcharge bill is still arriving on a delay.

The New Number Is 76 Cents Per Mile

The strongest energy story today is not another abstract crude chart. It is a line item in the grain freight bill. In its November 5 Grain Transportation Report, USDA said the average Class I railroad fuel surcharge for grain reached $0.76 per mile in November, an all-time record and 231 percent above the April 2026 level. That is not a forecast. That is the bill shippers are working with now. USDA’s Grain Transportation Report lays it out in the feature article on rail fuel surcharges.

Put it in plain English: a 1,000-mile grain haul now carries roughly $760 per railcar in average fuel surcharge before you even get to the base tariff. A 100-car movement turns that into about $76,000 of fuel surcharge exposure. Actual bills vary by railroad, route and tariff structure, because of course they do. Freight pricing could never be that merciful. But the arithmetic is not subtle.

This matters because grain does not move itself. Corn, soybeans and wheat leave farms by truck, rail, barge and ocean vessel. When diesel spikes, the first pain is obvious at the pump. The second pain is quieter: it gets embedded into transport formulas, bids, basis, export competition and eventually food costs. If you want the consumer version of the same problem, Notavello has been tracking why diesel is where the oil shock stops being abstract.

The bottom line: USDA says November grain rail fuel surcharges hit a record 76 cents per mile. That is how a diesel shock becomes a farm-margin problem, then a food-price problem, without needing a dramatic shortage headline.

Diesel Fell, But Not Enough To Save The Freight Bill

The annoying part is that diesel did move down. The latest EIA retail fuel update shows the U.S. average on-highway diesel price at $6.292 per gallon for November 2, 2026, down 6 cents from the prior week. That sounds like relief until you look one column over: it was still $2.539 per gallon above the same week last year. California was still above $8 a gallon, the Midwest above $6.40 and the West Coast above $7.27. EIA’s Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update is the clean source here.

USDA’s report uses that same diesel series because rail fuel surcharges are commonly indexed to DOE/EIA on-highway diesel prices. The catch is timing. USDA says grain rail fuel surcharges are generally updated monthly and often based on diesel prices from two months prior. That means September’s diesel pain is still showing up in November’s rail paperwork.

So yes, a weekly diesel dip helps. No, it does not erase the bill already coded into the surcharge schedule. Energy markets can fall on Monday and still leave agriculture paying September’s invoice in November. This is why crude-price relief and grocery-price relief do not arrive in the same truck.

Why Rail Surcharges Hit Farms Differently

Rail fuel surcharges are not just a railroad accounting curiosity. USDA notes that base tariff rates for grain are typically updated once a year, while fuel surcharges update monthly. That makes the fuel surcharge the moving part of the bill. When diesel goes from expensive to absurd, the surcharge becomes the part everyone suddenly reads.

USDA’s details show how uneven the system is. BNSF’s November 2026 grain fuel surcharge is listed at $0.61 per mile. Union Pacific’s is $0.84. Norfolk Southern’s is $0.95. Legacy Kansas City Southern and Canadian National’s U.S. program are listed at $1.04 per mile. Different strike prices, step functions and mileage assumptions produce different pain from the same diesel market.

One example in the USDA report is especially useful because it is boring in exactly the right way. On wheat moving from Salina, Kansas, to Houston export terminals, USDA says BNSF now applies a lower per-mile surcharge than Union Pacific, but BNSF’s total surcharge is higher because its measured route mileage is longer: 1,136 miles versus 765 miles. Last month BNSF was $9 per car cheaper on that surcharge. This month it is $50 per car more expensive. Same commodity, same broad origin-destination idea, different route math, different bill.

That is the kind of detail that does not fit into a cable-news oil segment. It is also the kind of detail that changes what an elevator can bid a farmer for grain.

The Grain Network Is Already Paying Up

USDA’s broader grain transportation indicators show the fuel shock is not isolated to one rail formula. For the week ending November 4, the truck cost index was 237 on a 2017 base of 100, compared with 142 a year earlier. Barge was 221, up from 185. Gulf ocean vessel costs were 190, up from 145, and Pacific ocean vessel costs were 188, up from 141. Rail, interestingly, was 128 versus 130 a year earlier, but the current record fuel surcharge is sitting inside the total route-specific math, not floating above it like a cartoon thundercloud.

USDA also reported that barged grain movements for the week ending October 31 totaled 282,400 tons, down 47 percent from the prior week and 68 percent from the same week last year. Do not overread that as a diesel-only story; river logistics have their own special talent for making everyone miserable. But it does show why shippers cannot simply wave at rail and say, “Fine, use the river.” The alternatives have prices, constraints and timing problems too.

That is where ordinary people enter the story. High grain freight costs can lower local cash bids for farmers, raise delivered costs for processors and exporters, or both. The exact split depends on contracts, competition, basis and who has bargaining power that week. But the cost has to land somewhere. Spoiler: it rarely evaporates out of kindness.

The War Risk Is Still In The Background, Not Gone

This rail surcharge record is not a generic war recap. The current, measurable development is the freight bill. Still, the backdrop matters because diesel did not get here by magic. EIA’s October Short-Term Energy Outlook said September retail diesel averaged $6.29 per gallon, driven by higher crude prices and rising crack spreads. EIA also said distillate inventories were expected to remain below the 2021-2025 average through the forecast period, keeping diesel crack spreads elevated and retail diesel above $4 per gallon through 2027. The STEO is worth reading if you enjoy charts that politely ruin your morning.

The regional-risk tape has not exactly turned calm either. Over the weekend, Saudi Arabia said it intercepted missiles, with falling debris damaging an Aramco building in Riyadh; the same AP report noted crew rescues after a cargo ship fire in the Sea of Oman and a tanker fire report near Khasab, Oman. Saudi officials reported no casualties at the Aramco facility. That is important: the point is not to invent an outage. The point is that insurance, routing, refinery risk and distillate supply remain vulnerable even when the headline oil price backs off. AP’s latest Middle East update supports the risk context without needing to pretend every incident is a supply collapse.

What To Watch Next

The next useful checks are not mysterious. Watch EIA’s weekly diesel price, especially the national number and regional numbers for the Midwest, Gulf Coast and West Coast. Watch distillate inventories, because diesel prices are not just crude prices in a fake mustache. Watch USDA’s Grain Transportation Report for whether December rail surcharges actually ease after October diesel averaged slightly below September.

The cleanest near-term test is simple: if diesel stays near $6, rail surcharges remain a farm-cost problem even if they come down from November’s record. If diesel falls but distillate inventories stay thin, the decline may be slow and patchy. If Gulf, Hormuz or Red Sea risk flares again, the freight bill gets another excuse to stay ugly.

The practical takeaway is also simple. Do not read falling crude as falling food pressure. Grain moves through contracts, tariffs, surcharges, river rates, export vessel rates and local basis. The record 76-cent-per-mile rail surcharge is the current receipt. It says the energy shock is no longer just at the pump. It is riding with the crop.
