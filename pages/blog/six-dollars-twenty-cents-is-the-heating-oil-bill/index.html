Six Dollars And Twenty Cents Is The Heating Oil Bill

The most useful oil-market number this morning is not another map of the Strait of Hormuz. It is $6.202 a gallon: the latest EIA average retail heating-oil price for the U.S. East Coast.

The Number To Watch Is $6.202 A Gallon

As this is published on October 28, 2026, the latest federal heating-oil survey is still the Energy Information Administration’s October 19 reading. It shows East Coast residential heating oil at $6.202 per gallon, up 5.2 cents in a week and up $2.666 per gallon from the same point last year. The U.S. average was barely lower, at $6.190 a gallon. Those figures are from EIA’s weekly Heating Oil and Propane Update, which is collected during the October-to-March heating season and published weekly after Wednesday afternoon Eastern time.

That makes today’s risk less theatrical than a tanker headline and more annoying in the way household bills are annoying. If a household bought 700 gallons at the East Coast average, the pretax fuel cost would be about $4,341. The year-over-year price difference alone would be about $1,866 for the same 700 gallons. That is not a forecast for every home. Tank size, insulation, weather, delivery timing, state taxes, service contracts and usage all matter. But it is a clean translation of the current EIA price into something more useful than a crude chart.

The source is not a dealer flyer or a panic post. EIA’s current table lists the October 19 East Coast residential heating-oil price at $6.202 a gallon and the year-ago increase at $2.666 a gallon: EIA Heating Oil and Propane Update.

The bottom line: Heating oil is where the crude shock walks up the driveway. East Coast households are entering winter with retail heating oil up $2.666 a gallon from a year ago and inventories still below normal.

This Is A Distillate Problem, Not Just A Heating-Oil Problem

Heating oil lives in the distillate family with diesel. That matters because the same broad fuel pool has to cover home heating, trucking, farm equipment, construction machinery, rail, backup generation and parts of industrial demand. When distillate is tight, the market does not politely reserve one bucket for households and another bucket for freight. It bids.

EIA’s October Short-Term Energy Outlook is blunt about the East Coast. It says heating-oil households, concentrated mostly in the Northeast, face prices more than 30% higher than last winter, while expected heating-oil expenditures rise 21% because EIA assumes a milder Northeast winter offsets part of the price hit. That is the polite version of the bill. The impolite version is that even a milder winter does not make a $6 handle feel normal.

The same EIA outlook says East Coast distillate inventories fell 32% below their five-year seasonal average in September and are expected to remain around 20% below the 2021–2025 average through the upcoming winter. That is why one cold snap, refinery problem, port delay or import disruption can matter fast. The margin is already thin before the weather starts doing weather things.

If this sounds familiar, it is the same basic vulnerability behind Notavello’s earlier look at the East Coast heating oil gap. The difference now is that the retail price has caught up to the inventory warning in a way households can see on a delivery ticket.

The Import Cushion Is Only So Big

The East Coast does not make enough distillate locally to meet all of its demand. EIA says the region supplements production with shipments from the Gulf Coast, including Colonial Pipeline barrels and waterborne shipments, plus imports from other countries. In 2025, the East Coast imported 124,000 barrels per day of distillate fuel oil, about 10% of regional consumption. During December through February, imported distillate typically accounts for about 15% of regional consumption.

That import dependence is the quiet chokepoint. The East Coast can ask the market for more barrels. The market can answer with higher prices, slower deliveries or a shrug. EIA’s October outlook says low global distillate inventories mean East Coast importers will have difficulty boosting imports enough to bring inventories closer to normal during the winter heating season. That is not a claim that homes are about to run out. It is a claim that the usual relief valve is stiff.

There is also a timing problem. Heating oil demand does not wait for a perfect logistics window. If cargoes are delayed, if freight gets more expensive, or if overseas refiners keep more diesel at home, the local dealer still has to fill tanks in Maine, New York, Pennsylvania, Connecticut and Rhode Island. A furnace does not care that the global market is complicated. It wants fuel, which is rude but consistent.

EIA’s October Short-Term Energy Outlook gives the import and inventory context directly: EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook, October 2026.

War Risk Is Showing Up In The Fuel Rack

The current price is not only about cold weather. The oil market is still digesting Middle East disruption, tanker risk and tight diesel supply. On October 23, the Associated Press reported Brent crude settled at $116.72 a barrel, up 3.1% on the day and at its highest level since June 2022. AP also reported that Brent had climbed from less than $97 at the start of October. That is the crude side of the bill.

The product side can be worse. Refineries do not magically turn every barrel of crude into the exact fuel buyers want. Diesel and heating oil depend on refinery configuration, operating rates, maintenance schedules, sulfur specs, blending economics, and regional logistics. When crude rises and distillate inventories are thin, heating oil can move like it has its own bad attitude.

That is why a Gulf shipping story becomes a Northeast household story. The chain looks like this:

  • Middle East risk raises the price of crude and the cost of moving oil and fuels.
  • Refiners pay more for crude and chase high-value products such as diesel and heating oil.
  • Distillate inventories start winter below normal on the East Coast.
  • Importers face a tight global market when they try to pull in replacement barrels.
  • Households see the result in the delivered heating-oil price.

AP’s October 23 market report is useful here because it ties the crude move to the broader Iran-war oil shock without requiring anyone to pretend every household studies futures curves for fun: AP on rising oil prices and Brent at $116.72.

What This Means Before The First Real Cold Snap

The practical consequence is simple: buyers with heating-oil exposure have less room for procrastination. That does not mean everyone should panic-fill at any price. It means the decision is now a risk-management problem, not just a seasonal chore.

For households, the questions are boring but useful. How much oil is actually in the tank? Is the gauge reliable? Is there a delivery contract or only will-call service? Does the household qualify for state heating assistance, and are applications open? Is the furnace tuned, or is it burning expensive fuel badly? None of this fixes global distillate tightness. It does reduce the odds of discovering the problem on the coldest night of the week, which is when expensive things like to introduce themselves.

For farms, small businesses and local governments, the same math applies with bigger numbers. School districts, snow-removal fleets, municipal buildings, warehouses and rural businesses that use distillate-linked fuels have to budget against a winter market that is already tight. The danger is not only the posted price. It is the cash-flow squeeze when multiple fuel-sensitive costs move together: delivery, freight, backup generation, equipment operation and heating.

The important restraint is this: the data does not prove a physical heating-oil shortage today. EIA is not saying the East Coast is empty. It is saying inventories are below normal, import relief is harder, and prices are already much higher than last winter. That is enough. A shortage is a dramatic word. A $6.202 gallon is a bill.

The Household Oil Shock Is Already Here

The easiest mistake is to wait for a cleaner headline: a closed strait, an emergency release, a formal shortage, a refinery outage with a name. Real fuel stress often arrives before that. It shows up as a dealer quote that seems absurd, a delivery minimum that hurts, a farm budget that stops balancing, or a town fuel line item that suddenly needs a meeting.

That is what makes the October 19 EIA heating-oil survey worth watching on October 28. It is current enough to matter, specific enough to price, and plain enough to understand. East Coast residential heating oil is $6.202 a gallon. It is up $2.666 from a year earlier. Inventories are below normal. Imports are an important winter cushion, and EIA says that cushion will be difficult to expand enough to normalize stocks.

Oil shocks usually start as geopolitical abstraction. This one is already in gallons.
