Three Hundred Forty Three Million Is The Farm Bill

The oil shock is no longer just a chart on a trading desk. In Arkansas, extension economists have put it into a farm budget: about $343 million in added fuel and fertilizer costs.

Arkansas Just Put A Price Tag On The Chokepoint

The cleanest energy story today is not another vague warning about the Strait of Hormuz. It is a state-level farm budget.

The University of Arkansas Division of Agriculture says war-restricted fuel and fertilizer shipments through the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea are expected to cost Arkansas farmers more than $343 million this year. That number is not an oil-market abstraction. It is diesel for tractors and pumps, highway fuel for trucks, and urea for crops that need nitrogen.

The estimate matters because it gives ordinary people a better translation layer. When crude routes are threatened, the first bill does not always arrive as a gasoline sign. Sometimes it arrives as a rice budget, a corn budget, a cotton budget, and eventually a grocery bill with no little label saying “shipping chokepoint surcharge.” That would be too helpful.

Notavello has been tracking this same pressure in fertilizer and fuel markets before, including in the fall ammonia and farm-cost story. The Arkansas analysis is different because it assigns the shock to acres and crops, not just benchmarks.

The bottom line: Shipping risk is now showing up as diesel, urea, and crop-margin pain. Arkansas is a useful warning because it turns a global chokepoint into dollars per acre.

The Diesel Budget Broke First

Arkansas’ 2026 crop budgets were built with farm diesel at $2.46 per gallon. According to the University of Arkansas analysis, the 2026 year-to-date farm diesel average had risen to $3.84, while the March 1 through September 30 average was $4.22, or 72 percent above budget.

The harvest-season number is worse. The same analysis says farm diesel averaged $4.72 so far during harvest, 92 percent above budget. As of October 5, the current farm diesel price cited by the economists was $5.47, while highway diesel used in farm trucks and delivery was $5.94.

That produces very different pain by crop. The added diesel cost alone works out to:

  • $34.22 per acre for soybeans.
  • $37.44 per acre for cotton.
  • $40.33 per acre for corn.
  • $49.61 per acre for peanuts.
  • $74.94 per acre for rice.

Rice gets hit hard because it is fuel-hungry. Irrigation and field work are not optional details. A crop budget is not a vibes document; it is where the bank, the seed dealer, the fuel supplier, and the weather all meet for a little argument.

Urea Turns The Fuel Shock Into A Crop Shock

Diesel is only half the Arkansas story. The other half is nitrogen fertilizer, especially urea. The state’s 2026 budget assumed urea at $560 per ton. By May, University of Arkansas economists said urea prices had risen roughly 39 percent.

That increase added $37.39 per acre for cotton, $43.75 for rice, and $57.40 for corn. Soybeans and peanuts were treated differently because they are legumes and can fix their own nitrogen, so the analysis did not assign them urea-related expenses.

Combine the marginal diesel and urea increases, and the per-acre pain becomes more visible:

  • $118.69 per acre for rice.
  • $97.73 per acre for corn.
  • $74.83 per acre for cotton.
  • $49.61 per acre for peanuts.
  • $34.22 per acre for soybeans.

This is why fertilizer belongs in an energy story. Nitrogen fertilizer is tied to natural gas, ammonia, shipping, and industrial energy costs. When Middle East shipping and gas flows get messy, fertilizer does not politely wait in a separate aisle. It comes along and makes the crop budget worse.

The Global Fertilizer Signal Is Still Ugly

The Arkansas numbers fit the broader global picture. The World Bank reported that its fertilizer price index rose more than 12 percent in the first quarter of 2026, reaching its highest level since October 2022 by April. It also said nitrogen urea prices climbed above $850 per metric ton in April, up 80 percent since February.

The World Bank’s explanation is not subtle. It pointed to export disruptions linked to the Strait of Hormuz, production outages, and tighter supply. It also noted that the Middle East accounts for nearly one-quarter of global urea exports. That is the part people miss when they talk about Hormuz only as an oil route. It is also a fertilizer route, a sulfur route, an ammonia route, and a food-cost route.

The World Bank projected urea prices to rise nearly 60 percent in 2026 before easing in 2027, while warning that prolonged shipping disruption or higher input costs could push prices higher. Forecasts are not guarantees, but this one explains why farmers are not treating the current spike as a one-week inconvenience.

Diesel Tightness Is Not Helping Anyone

The U.S. diesel backdrop is also tight. In its October 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook, the U.S. Energy Information Administration said U.S. retail diesel averaged $6.29 per gallon in September, driven by higher crude prices and wider crack spreads. EIA also said East Coast distillate inventories were 32 percent below their five-year seasonal average in September.

That does not mean every farm pays the same price as the national highway diesel average. Farm diesel, dyed diesel, clear highway diesel, bulk contracts, taxes, and regional supply all differ. But the direction is the point. The national diesel system is tight enough that farm budgets in Arkansas are being rewritten in real time.

Arkansas has already moved to reduce some pressure. The University of Arkansas article notes that Governor Sarah Huckabee Sanders signed an executive order temporarily allowing farmers and loggers to use red-dyed diesel in highway vehicles through late October 2026. That can help with trucking costs, but it does not erase the main bill. If your budget assumed $2.46 diesel and harvest fuel is far above that, a tax and compliance workaround is relief, not a time machine.

Why This Matters Beyond Arkansas

The state-level total came from multiplying added per-acre costs by planted acreage. University of Arkansas cited USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service acreage figures showing soybeans at 3.45 million acres, all rice at 901,000 acres, corn at 760,000 acres, cotton at 560,000 acres, and peanuts at 40,000 acres. The resulting added cost estimate was $343.18 million.

That total breaks down to $118.1 million for soybeans, $106.9 million for rice, $74.3 million for corn, $41.9 million for cotton, and $2.0 million for peanuts. Soybeans take the biggest total hit because acreage is huge. Rice takes a brutal per-acre hit because fuel and fertilizer intensity are higher. Different crops, same unpleasant math.

The consumer consequence is not automatic overnight food inflation. Farmers can absorb some costs, delay purchases, change applications, switch crop plans next season, or eat margin they did not have. Processors, shippers, retailers, and exporters all complicate the path from acre to shelf. Still, higher input costs have a way of becoming someone’s price, someone’s debt, or someone’s lower production.

That is the useful lesson from Arkansas. The Iran war and shipping disruptions do not need to close every route to matter. They only need to make fuel and fertilizer unreliable enough that budgets stop working. Once that happens, the war premium is not theoretical. It is sitting in the field, burning diesel.
