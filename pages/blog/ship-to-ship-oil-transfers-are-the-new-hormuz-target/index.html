Ship-To-Ship Oil Transfers Are The New Hormuz Target

The latest Strait of Hormuz problem is not a formal closure. It is the uglier version: the workaround itself is being shot at.

The Workaround Is Now In The Line Of Fire

The important development this week is not another grand claim about the Strait of Hormuz being open or closed. It is narrower and more useful: commercial vessels tied to ship-to-ship oil movements are now part of the target set.

On October 13, the Associated Press reported that Iran appeared to be targeting commercial vessels conducting ship-to-ship oil transfers in and near the Strait of Hormuz, citing the Institute for the Study of War. AP also reported that the U.K. Maritime Trade Operations Centre said at least three vessels had been attacked by unknown projectiles in the strait a day earlier. That is the kind of sentence that looks like maritime wallpaper until you remember what ship-to-ship transfers do. They are the plumbing behind the workaround. AP’s October 13 report is the cleanest current read on the shift.

Ship-to-ship transfers let oil move from one vessel to another without using a normal port call. In this crisis, that can mean smaller or more expendable vessels moving cargo through dangerous water before the oil is transferred to larger tankers waiting farther out. It is not elegant. It is not cheap. But it can keep barrels moving when the normal route is politically, militarily or commercially poisonous.

Target that system, and the question changes. It is no longer only “can a tanker pass through Hormuz?” It becomes “can cargo be assembled, transferred, insured, crewed and delivered without every handoff becoming a new risk event?” That is a much worse question. Naturally, it is the one the market now gets to answer with money.

The bottom line: Ship-to-ship oil transfers were supposed to keep Gulf barrels moving when direct transits became too risky. If those transfers become targets, the fuel bill gets a new surcharge.

Hormuz Volumes Already Fell Before This Week

The reason this matters is measurable. The U.S. Energy Information Administration’s energy security data show total oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz at 21.6 million barrels per day in the fourth quarter of 2025, then 14.9 million barrels per day in the first quarter of 2026, and 4.9 million barrels per day in the second quarter of 2026. LNG flows through Hormuz fell from 10.5 billion cubic feet per day in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 0.8 billion cubic feet per day in the second quarter of 2026. The EIA also warns that AIS ship-tracking data around Hormuz has been especially unreliable since the end of February 2026, so the numbers are being revised as better route information comes in. See EIA’s global energy security tables.

That caveat is important. It does not make the data useless. It makes the physical market harder to see, which is exactly the point. When ships turn off transponders, take odd routes, wait offshore, use transfers, or avoid obvious reporting trails, the oil may still exist, but the supply chain becomes slower and harder to price. A barrel that arrives after three extra handoffs is not the same barrel as one that clears a normal export terminal on a normal tanker under normal insurance.

This is why a few projectile strikes can matter more than their tonnage suggests. One damaged tanker is an incident. A repeated pattern near the transfer system is a tax on the workaround. Charterers need different ships. Insurers widen exclusions or raise premiums. Crews become harder to assign. Terminals and buyers demand more documentation. Cargoes sit. None of that requires a Hollywood-style blockade. It just requires enough uncertainty to make each voyage less boring.

The Price Signal Shows Up In Diesel Before It Shows Up In Speeches

The first consumer-facing signal is usually not crude oil itself. It is diesel, heating oil and jet fuel. Those are distillates, and distillates are where a shipping disruption becomes a truck bill, a farm bill, a delivery surcharge and a winter heating bill.

The EIA’s October Short-Term Energy Outlook says retail distillate prices are expected to remain above $6 per gallon in October, then come down gradually as crude prices and inventories improve. It also says U.S. distillate inventories are expected to remain below the 2021–2025 average through the forecast period, keeping diesel crack spreads elevated and retail prices above $4 per gallon through 2027. That is not a panic forecast. It is worse in a way: it is a baseline forecast with the pain already baked in. The EIA’s petroleum product outlook lays it out in plain numbers at its October STEO petroleum products page.

The East Coast is especially exposed because it consumes more distillate than it produces. EIA says East Coast distillate inventories were 32% below their 2021–2025 seasonal average in September, and it expects the region to remain 20% to 30% below average through the coming winter. Imports covered about 10% of East Coast distillate consumption in 2025, rising to about 15% during the December-to-February peak. That is the part worth underlining: the region that needs winter heating oil also needs imports just when global distillate is tight.

So, yes, a tanker incident thousands of miles away can become a Northeast household problem. Not by magic. By diesel math. The same barrel family feeds heating oil tanks, freight lanes, construction equipment, farm machines and delivery fleets. When the workaround gets expensive, everyone downstream gets invited to pay. Very democratic, in the least charming sense.

Ship-To-Ship Transfers Are Not Free Capacity

There is a tempting mistake here: treating ship-to-ship transfers as a spare lane, like opening another checkout register. That is not how it works.

A transfer operation needs the right weather, the right anchorage, the right crews, compatible vessels, hoses, fenders, tugs or support craft, inspectors, documentation, sanction screening, and insurance. It also needs a buyer willing to accept the chain of custody. In a quiet market, those details are annoying. In a shooting market, they become the market.

The dry version is this: the marginal barrel has more steps. The plain-English version is better: the oil is not just moving from A to B anymore. It is playing hopscotch under threat. Every hop adds cost and time. Every delay ties up ships. Every tied-up ship tightens freight. Every higher freight quote makes the delivered fuel price worse, even if the crude benchmark has a calm day on a trading screen.

This is also why the crisis has lasted longer in bills than in headlines. A dramatic strike gets attention for a day. A damaged transfer network changes behavior for weeks. Charterers do not forget quickly. Neither do crews. Neither do insurers, whose entire business model is remembering unpleasant things and invoicing accordingly.

Notavello has covered the earlier version of this problem in the Hormuz shuttle tanker freight shock. This week’s development is the next stage: the shuttle system itself is being squeezed.

What Ordinary Buyers Should Watch Now

Most people do not buy crude cargoes, book Suezmax tankers or negotiate war-risk clauses. Lucky you. But you can still watch the right public signals instead of waiting for a vague “Middle East tensions” headline to explain your fuel bill after the fact.

  • East Coast distillate stocks: EIA’s weekly series showed PADD 1 distillate inventories at 21.687 million barrels for the week ending October 2, 2026. That is the number to watch as cold weather gets closer.
  • Retail diesel: if diesel stays stubborn even when crude softens, the problem is refining, inventories, freight, or all of the above. Usually all of the above, because markets enjoy group projects.
  • UKMTO incident frequency: one-off attacks matter less than clusters near the same chokepoint, anchorage or transfer lane.
  • Insurance and freight commentary: war-risk premiums are boring until they are embedded in every delivered cargo.
  • Import dependence: regions that need imported distillate in winter have less room to pretend global shipping risk is someone else’s problem.

The useful framing is not “will Hormuz close?” That question is too binary for a messy system. The better question is whether the workarounds are becoming slow, risky and expensive enough to act like a partial closure. Right now, ship-to-ship transfers are where that answer is being tested.

The Market Is Pricing A System, Not A Headline

The blunt conclusion is that the Strait of Hormuz does not need to be completely blocked to raise costs. It only has to become unreliable at the points where cargoes are collected, transferred and handed off. This week’s reports point directly at those points.

That is why the story belongs in fuel bills, not just foreign-policy columns. A projectile strike on a transfer-linked vessel can ripple into tanker availability, insurance, cargo timing, refinery feedstock planning, diesel inventories and heating oil prices. The chain is long, but it is not mysterious.

The cheerful version would be that markets adapt. They do. The less cheerful version is that adaptation sends you the invoice. Ship-to-ship transfers were part of the adaptation. Now they are part of the risk.
