ADNOC Vessel Attack Keeps Hormuz Risk In Fuel Prices As U.S. Inventories Build

ADNOC L&S reported a vessel attack in the Strait of Hormuz, while oil prices fell on fresh U.S. inventory data. That is the awkward shape of this market: more barrels on paper, more risk on the water.

ADNOC Confirmed A Hormuz Attack, Not A Rumor

On November 18, 2026, ADNOC Logistics & Services said one of its vessels had been attacked while transiting the Strait of Hormuz on the evening of November 17. Reuters reported that one crew member was missing, 11 were injured and the rest of the crew had been evacuated. Maritime security sources also told Reuters that the crew of a liquefied petroleum gas tanker abandoned ship after the vessel was struck by a projectile while sailing through the strait. That is not a market whisper. That is a working energy corridor taking another hit.

The development matters because ADNOC L&S is not a fringe operator running a mystery ship through a bad neighborhood. It is the logistics arm tied to Abu Dhabi’s state oil system. When a vessel connected to that ecosystem is attacked in Hormuz, the market does not need much poetry to understand the message: shipping risk is still live where Gulf energy leaves the region.

You can read the Reuters account of the ADNOC L&S attack here: Reuters on the ADNOC L&S vessel attack in Hormuz. The important caveat is also in that report: Reuters could not independently verify every claim made by Iranian military sources about other ships catching fire. Good. Markets need facts, not fog machines.

The bottom line: A U.S. inventory build can cool crude prices. It cannot make a tanker route safe. Until Hormuz risk fades, diesel, grain freight and import costs keep carrying a war premium.

Oil Fell Because Inventory Data Pointed The Other Way

The same morning, oil prices moved lower. Reuters reported Brent crude at $92.53 a barrel and WTI at $84.77 at 0550 GMT on November 18. The immediate pressure came from American Petroleum Institute data cited by market sources: U.S. crude stocks rose by 5.28 million barrels in the week ended November 13, gasoline stocks rose by 5.12 million barrels and distillate inventories rose by 351,000 barrels.

That sounds bearish because it is bearish, at least for the next price screen. More crude and fuel in storage means traders have less reason to panic-buy every headline. But the same Reuters report said continued U.S.-Iran tensions and shipping risk through Hormuz limited the price decline. That is the part worth underlining. Inventory builds answer the question, “Do we have barrels?” Vessel attacks answer a different question: “Can we move them without turning a tanker crew into the day’s casualty count?”

The market is therefore stuck with two truths that do not cancel each other out. First, U.S. inventories can rise. Second, a major energy chokepoint can remain dangerous. If you only watch the crude settlement price, you miss the second half. If you only watch war headlines, you miss why prices do not go straight up every morning. Congratulations, the spreadsheet and the sea lane are fighting again.

Hormuz Flows Are Still Far Below Normal

The reason Hormuz headlines carry so much weight is simple: before the war disruption, the strait handled enormous energy volumes. The U.S. Energy Information Administration’s latest energy-security data show total oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz at 21.1 million barrels per day in 2Q25 and 21.4 million barrels per day in 3Q25. By 2Q26, EIA put that flow at 5.1 million barrels per day, before a partial rebound to 9.1 million barrels per day in 3Q26. LNG flows through Hormuz fell from 11.0 billion cubic feet per day in 2Q25 to 0.8 billion cubic feet per day in 2Q26, then recovered only to 1.4 billion cubic feet per day in 3Q26.

Those figures are why a single vessel attack does not stay “over there.” The strait is not a scenic shortcut. It is a valve for crude, refined products and LNG. EIA’s Hormuz table is here: EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook energy-security data.

That partial recovery matters, too. Hormuz is not fully closed. Gulf exports have not vanished. But the gap between 21 million barrels per day before the crisis and 9.1 million barrels per day in 3Q26 is not a rounding error. It is a missing-routes problem, a tanker-insurance problem, a refinery-feedstock problem and eventually a household-budget problem. The fuel market can tolerate risk. It charges for it first.

Diesel Relief Is Real, But It Is Not Normal

The U.S. retail diesel number finally looks less ridiculous than it did earlier in the fall. EIA’s November 17 fuel update put the U.S. average on-highway diesel price at $5.937 a gallon for the week of November 16, down 19 cents from the prior week. That is welcome. It is also still $2.069 above the same week a year earlier. Gasoline showed the same pattern in smaller type: regular gasoline averaged $3.805 a gallon, down 19 cents on the week but still 74.3 cents above a year earlier.

The official EIA fuel table is here: EIA Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update. It is useful because it keeps the conversation honest. A weekly decline is not the same as cheap fuel. It is just a decline from a very high ledge.

Stocks tell the same story. In the Weekly Petroleum Status Report for the week ended November 6, EIA put U.S. distillate fuel oil stocks at 103.3 million barrels. That was up 4.1 million barrels from the prior week, but still 7.6 million barrels, or 6.8%, below the comparable week in 2025. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve stood at 279.5 million barrels, down from 282.2 million the prior week and far below 410.4 million a year earlier. Inventory builds help. Thin cushions still matter.

The Food Channel Is Already Showing The Fuel Strain

For ordinary people, the clearest path from Hormuz to the kitchen is diesel. Trucks burn it. Farm equipment burns it. Barges and ocean freight price around fuel and availability. Railroads add fuel surcharges. None of that appears on your receipt as “Strait of Hormuz surcharge,” because grocery receipts are rude but not that honest.

USDA’s November 12 Grain Transportation Report shows the pressure plainly. For the week ending November 11, USDA’s grain transportation cost index put truck costs at 231, with 2017 as the base year of 100. One year earlier, that truck index was 145. Barge costs were 199 versus 172 a year earlier. Gulf ocean vessel costs were 186 versus 142. Pacific ocean vessel costs were 188 versus 135. USDA explains that the truck indicator is based on diesel prices, while the rail indicator includes tariff rates and fuel surcharges for selected shuttle train routes.

The USDA report is here: USDA Grain Transportation Report, November 12, 2026. Its Mexico landed-cost section also shows how transport sits inside food trade. In third-quarter 2026, USDA listed Illinois corn shipped by water route to Veracruz at $262.33 per metric ton, including $21.44 for truck, $37.75 for barge and $23.49 for ocean freight. Iowa corn shipped by land route to a U.S.-Mexico border location totaled $249.06 per metric ton, including $7.55 for truck and $65.40 for rail.

That does not mean fuel alone sets food prices. Farm values, weather, currency and demand all get a vote. But fuel is one of the channels that turns a shipping war into a supermarket annoyance. If you run a farm, a trucking business, a food distributor or just a household, you do not need Brent at $150 to feel it. You need diesel to stay expensive long enough for the surcharge machinery to keep clicking.

What To Watch Before Calling This Relief

The next useful check is the official EIA petroleum inventory release, because API data is industry-reported and preliminary. If EIA confirms a large crude and gasoline build, crude prices may get more breathing room. If it does not, the market will reprice the morning’s optimism quickly. Oil traders are not sentimental people. Handy, in this case.

For everyone else, the practical watch list is shorter:

  • Hormuz vessel incidents: one more tanker attack can matter more than one more inventory estimate.
  • Distillate stocks: diesel, heating oil and jet fuel share the distillate family, and the latest official stock level was still below last year.
  • Retail diesel: the $5.937 national average is down, but it is nowhere near normal freight math.
  • Grain freight indicators: USDA’s truck, barge and ocean indexes show whether fuel relief is reaching food logistics or merely making headlines.
  • SPR movement: the reserve is still being used as a cushion while commercial stocks do the day-to-day work.

This is why the ADNOC attack belongs in the same story as a U.S. inventory build. One is about danger on the route. The other is about barrels in storage. You need both to understand why crude can fall while fuel costs remain stubborn. Notavello has been tracking the same chokepoint logic in earlier energy coverage, including Hormuz shipping-lane screening risk. The short version has not changed: when the route is risky, the pump and the freight invoice do not calm down just because one data point improved.
