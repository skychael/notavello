BLS Says Truck Freight Prices Rose 3.9% While Diesel Stayed Above $6

The new inflation signal is not at the gasoline pump. It is in the truck lane, where BLS says freight prices jumped again while diesel remained painfully expensive.

BLS Put The Pressure Point In The Truck Lane

The concrete development today is simple: the Bureau of Labor Statistics said prices for truck transportation of freight rose 3.9% in October. That single line mattered more than the headline inflation number because BLS said truck freight accounted for more than 80% of the October increase in final demand services. The full BLS Producer Price Index release also showed final demand transportation and warehousing services up 2.0% for the month.

That is not a niche trucking problem. It is the freight layer under groceries, building materials, replacement parts, farm inputs, medicine, restaurant supplies and pretty much every boring thing you only notice when it gets expensive. A 3.9% monthly move in truck freight is not background noise. It is the sound of fuel costs being passed through the economy with a clipboard and a surcharge table.

The headline Producer Price Index rose 0.3% in October, down sharply from September’s 1.4% increase. If you stop there, the report looks like relief. Do not stop there. The same report put the 12-month PPI increase at 6.5%, and final demand foods rose 1.3% in October. Food does not need every input to rise at once. It only needs enough pressure from freight, packaging, refrigeration, labor and financing to keep margins from absorbing the hit forever.

The bottom line: Diesel has eased, not normalized. Truck freight prices rose 3.9% in October, and that is how an oil shock keeps moving into food, retail and farm costs after crude headlines cool off.

Diesel Fell, But It Did Not Become Cheap

The awkward part is that diesel prices did decline in the latest weekly retail data. EIA’s Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update put the U.S. on-highway diesel average at $6.127 per gallon on November 9, down 16.5 cents from the prior week. That is a real drop. It is also still $2.29 above the same week a year earlier.

This is why the freight number matters. Businesses do not plan routes, bids and delivered prices around the emotional relief of “down from last week.” They plan around the level. A national diesel average above $6 means refrigerated trailers, long-haul dry vans, harvest hauling, parcel networks and local delivery fleets are still operating in a fuel environment that would have looked absurd before the Iran-war oil shock.

Gasoline has slipped below $4 nationally, which matters to households. But diesel is the fuel inside the goods system. If gasoline is what you see on the station sign, diesel is what is embedded in the shelf price. Notavello has been tracking this distinction for months; the older warning in diesel is where the oil shock stops being abstract is now showing up in official wholesale inflation data.

The Distillate Cushion Improved, Not Enough To Relax

EIA’s latest Weekly Petroleum Status Report shows U.S. distillate fuel oil stocks at 103.3 million barrels for the week ending November 6. That was a 4.1 million barrel weekly build from 99.3 million barrels, so the inventory picture did improve. Good. Take the win where it exists.

But the same EIA table shows distillate inventories still 7.6 million barrels below the year-earlier level, a 6.8% gap. Ultra-low-sulfur distillate, the category most relevant to modern diesel, stood at 91.8 million barrels, also below last year. The EIA weekly petroleum report is not screaming “empty tanks,” but it is also not showing a comfortable diesel market.

That distinction is important because bad energy writing loves false choices. Either everything is fine because inventories built for one week, or everything is collapsing because diesel is expensive. The actual read is more annoying: supply improved, but prices and freight costs are still high enough to matter. Annoying, sadly, is often the correct answer.

Hormuz Workarounds Helped Crude, But Freight Still Carries The Scar

The crude market has gotten some help from workarounds. EIA’s November Short-Term Energy Outlook said continued flows through the Strait of Hormuz using ship-to-ship transfers along Oman’s coast added supply, and Brent fell below $100 per barrel on November 4. That is a meaningful easing from October, when EIA said Brent averaged $124 per barrel after falling inventories, tanker attacks, constrained shipping capacity and higher transportation costs tightened supply.

Still, EIA did not call the problem solved. Its November global oil market outlook said heightened disruption risk and longer shipping routes would limit the near-term effect of global stock builds. EIA also forecast Brent near $100 per barrel for November and December, while assuming Middle East flows remain somewhat constrained.

That is the mechanism behind the sticky freight number. A barrel can move, but if it moves through a more expensive, slower, riskier system, the delivered fuel cost does not reset cleanly. Ship-to-ship transfers, bypass routes and rerouted tankers are better than shut-ins. They are not the same thing as a normal market. You can move the barrel and still leave a bruise on diesel, insurance, shipping capacity and refinery economics.

Why Food And Farm Costs Are Still Exposed

The October PPI report gives a useful map of where this can travel next. Final demand foods rose 1.3% in October. Food and alcohol wholesaling moved higher. Truck freight jumped. Fuels and lubricants retailing also moved higher. That combination does not guarantee a specific grocery-price increase next month, and nobody should pretend it does. But it does show the pipeline is still under pressure.

Farmers feel this twice. First, diesel is a direct operating cost for harvest, tillage, irrigation where diesel pumps are used, grain hauling and on-farm transport. Second, freight affects the delivered cost of seed, crop chemicals, replacement parts, fertilizer and feed. Even when fertilizer itself is not the day’s headline, the truck that brings it to the co-op does not run on vibes.

Retailers and food companies have a choice when freight rises: absorb it, shrink margins, renegotiate, delay promotions, raise prices or redesign supply chains. In calm markets, they can hide small changes for a while. In a year with diesel above $6 and wholesale goods already up sharply from a year ago, the hiding places are fewer.

The Number To Watch Is Not Just Crude

The clean headline would be “oil is down.” The useful headline is “truck freight is up.” Crude matters, obviously. But the October PPI data says the everyday consequence is now showing in transportation services, not just futures screens.

Watch three numbers from here. First, the EIA weekly diesel average: if it stays above $6, freight contracts and fuel surcharges stay hot. Second, distillate inventories: builds help, but the market needs more than one good week. Third, the monthly BLS truck transportation index: if another 3% or 4% increase appears, the fuel shock is still moving through the economy even if crude traders have found a calmer story.

For ordinary people, that means the oil war does not have to produce an official shortage to matter. It only has to keep diesel high enough for long enough that the cost of moving goods becomes part of the price of goods. That is exactly what the October freight data is warning.
