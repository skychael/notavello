IEA Says Hormuz Shock Removed 2.8B Barrels of Oil and 60 BCM of Gas

The International Energy Agency just put a hard number on the Hormuz shock: 2.8 billion barrels of oil and 60 billion cubic meters of gas supply lost from the Gulf. That is not a market wobble. That is the kind of disruption that crawls into freight rates, farm inputs, airfare and heating bills.

IEA Put a Number on the Disruption

The strongest energy development today is not another vague warning about the Strait of Hormuz. It is the International Energy Agency’s new World Energy Outlook, which says the 2026 disruption to energy flows through Hormuz is the largest energy supply shock the world has faced and, as of November, is still not over.

The IEA’s headline numbers are ugly in a useful way: cumulative losses of 2.8 billion barrels of oil and 60 billion cubic meters of gas supply from the Gulf. The agency says the system has shown resilience, but that the burden is increasingly falling on consumers and high prices as buffers are run down. You can read the IEA’s executive summary here: IEA World Energy Outlook 2026 executive summary.

That matters because it separates this from the usual war-market fog. A tanker incident can move a futures screen for a day. A multi-billion-barrel cumulative supply loss changes how refiners, airlines, farmers, chemical producers and governments behave. The boring phrase for that is “demand adjustment.” The plain-English version is: people buy less, pay more, substitute badly or wait.

The bottom line: The Hormuz crisis is no longer just about whether tankers can get through this week. The IEA says the shock has already drained massive oil and gas volumes, while emergency stock releases and bypass routes are doing triage, not restoring normal trade.

Hormuz Was Too Big to Simply Route Around

The Strait of Hormuz mattered before the war because it was not just another shipping lane. According to the IEA, about 20 million barrels per day of crude oil and oil products moved through the strait in 2025, equal to roughly 25% of world seaborne oil trade. The same factsheet says Qatar and the UAE LNG flows through Hormuz represented almost one-fifth of global LNG trade.

The escape routes are real but limited. The IEA says Saudi Arabia and the UAE have an estimated 3.5 million to 5.5 million barrels per day of available crude pipeline capacity that can potentially bypass the strait. That is helpful. It is not a magic tunnel under the Gulf. Other producers, including Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain and Iran, rely on Hormuz for the vast majority of their oil exports.

This is why the crisis keeps showing up in places far from the Gulf. Asia is more exposed than Europe because most Hormuz-linked oil and LNG goes east. But once diesel, jet fuel, petrochemical feedstocks and LNG become globally tighter, nobody gets a clean exemption. The United States can produce a lot of oil and still feel the price of a missing barrel if the missing product is diesel, a chemical feedstock or imported fertilizer material.

Diesel, Jet Fuel and Feedstocks Are the Real Pressure Points

The crude oil headline is big, but the more practical problem is refined products and feedstocks. The IEA says the largest consumer impacts have come through shortages of petrochemical feedstocks and liquefied petroleum gas for cooking, plus higher prices, especially for diesel and jet fuel. That is the line from the report worth taping to the dispatch desk.

Diesel is not just something truckers complain about on the radio. It moves groceries, parcel freight, farm equipment, construction machinery, mining trucks and backup generators. Jet fuel turns directly into airline operating pressure. Petrochemical feedstocks end up in plastics, packaging, chemicals and fertilizer chains. If you are looking for the place where an energy shock becomes a grocery shelf or farm input problem, this is it.

The IEA’s Middle East market page says Gulf crude exports had recovered to 15 million barrels per day in October, more than 80% of pre-war levels, helped by ship-to-ship transfers and alternative routes. That sounds reassuring until you read the next part: refined oil product markets remain particularly tight, Gulf refined product and LPG exports are roughly half of prewar levels, and diesel markets are under significant strain.

That is the annoying shape of this crisis. Crude can improve while the product market still pinches. You can have more barrels moving and still not have enough of the right molecules in the right port at the right time. Energy markets enjoy this kind of technical cruelty.

Emergency Stocks Bought Time, Not Normal

Emergency reserves have helped keep the shock from becoming worse. The IEA Governing Board said in October that about 330 million barrels had already been released under the March 2026 collective action, with roughly 100 million barrels still to be completed and more than 20 million barrels of diesel planned for release as soon as possible. That is a serious intervention, not a press-release garnish.

But emergency barrels are a bridge, not a refinery. They can smooth a panic, cover temporary gaps and buy governments time. They do not rebuild damaged LNG capacity, make Hormuz safe, replace petrochemical feedstocks or refill every national stockpile at the same time.

The U.S. inventory picture shows the tradeoff. In the EIA’s Weekly Petroleum Status Report for the week ending November 13, the Strategic Petroleum Reserve stood at 276.4 million barrels, down 32.7% from a year earlier. U.S. distillate fuel oil stocks rose to 104.9 million barrels, but they were still 5.6% below the year-earlier level. That is better than an outright draw. It is not a comfortable cushion.

This is the same reason recent diesel relief should be read carefully. Lower pump numbers help, but the stock base is still thin enough that a refinery outage, cold snap or another shipping disruption can matter quickly. For more context on why diesel relief is not the same as diesel abundance, see Notavello’s earlier look at diesel prices and tight EIA stocks.

The Farm and Food Link Is Not Theoretical

The IEA’s setting-the-scene chapter says the near closure of Hormuz also cut off supplies of fertilizers, aluminium, sulphur and helium, increasing risks for industry and food security. Fertilizer deserves special attention because it is where energy, shipping and food inflation hold hands in public.

Natural gas is the main feedstock for ammonia. Ammonia sits behind nitrogen fertilizer, including urea. Sulphur matters for phosphate fertilizer production. LNG disruptions also affect gas markets outside the Gulf, especially in import-dependent regions. When energy traders talk about molecules, farmers eventually see invoices.

This does not mean every country is about to run out of fertilizer. That would be too neat, and reality is rarely that courteous. It means the system has less slack. Buyers may need to pay up, delay purchases, change sourcing or accept lower application rates if pricing gets ugly. Each choice has a consequence: farm margins, crop yields, food processors’ input costs or consumer prices.

The useful thing about the IEA report is that it does not ask you to believe in vibes. It connects the chokepoint to physical supply losses, product shortages and feedstock stress. That is the chain that matters.

What to Watch Next

The next signal is not one dramatic headline. It is a cluster of boring indicators that together say whether the shock is easing or merely changing shape.

  • Distillate inventories: rising stocks are good, but the level matters more than one weekly build.
  • Refined product exports from the Gulf: crude recovery means less if diesel, LPG and petrochemical feedstocks remain constrained.
  • LNG availability: Qatar and UAE flows matter heavily for Asian gas buyers, and there are no simple marine detours for stranded LNG.
  • Emergency stock releases: each release reduces near-term pain but also lowers the emergency cushion for the next shock.
  • Fertilizer input pricing: ammonia, urea, sulphur and natural gas are the watchlist for food-price pressure.

The clean takeaway is this: the Hormuz crisis has moved beyond a tanker-count story. The IEA is now describing a structural supply shock large enough to change trade routes, drain emergency reserves and force consumers to absorb pain through prices and reduced demand. If you buy diesel, ship goods, fly, farm or eat food that traveled more than ten miles, that is your lane too. Sorry. Energy has never respected neat categories.
