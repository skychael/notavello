The Sohar Tanker Queue Is The Oil Bill

The oil market found a workaround for the war. Now the workaround has a queue, a tugboat problem, and a record tanker bill.

The Bottleneck Moved To Sohar

The clean version of the story says Gulf oil is still moving. The useful version says it is moving through a much more expensive and fragile machine.

Reuters reported on September 25 that ship-to-ship crude transfers in the Gulf of Oman have reached their limits after Saudi Arabia shifted more exports away from the Red Sea route and back through the Strait of Hormuz. The key place to watch is off Sohar, Oman, where crude loaded inside the Gulf is transferred onto larger tankers outside the strait. According to the report, Saudi Aramco sold more than 60 million barrels of crude for ship-to-ship transfer off Sohar for September and October after the East-West Pipeline attack halted Red Sea exports from Yanbu. Saudi crude exports via Hormuz were on track to rise to 3.6 million barrels per day in September, up from about 900,000 barrels per day in August, based on Kpler data cited by Reuters in gCaptain’s republication of the Reuters report.

That is the current development that matters. Not another map with arrows. Not another dramatic sentence about chokepoints. The practical problem is that a backup route has become a working port system without enough slack: tankers, transfer crews, tugboats, safe anchorage, scheduling, insurance and daylight-or-darkness operating windows. Oil can be available on paper and still arrive late, expensive or in the wrong place.

The bottom line: Saudi crude is moving again through Hormuz, but not cheaply. Ship-to-ship transfers off Oman are hitting capacity, and that freight bill eventually lands in fuel, freight, food and refinery margins.

A Supertanker Is Now The Scarce Part

The tanker math is blunt. A very large crude carrier, or VLCC, usually carries about 2 million barrels of crude. Reuters cited Kpler analyst Panagiotis Krontiras estimating that the nearly 3 million barrel-per-day increase in Saudi exports through Hormuz would require another 36 to 40 VLCCs. Oil Brokerage’s Anoop Singh put the additional VLCC need at 40 this month, up from 24 in August, with 15 ships needed for shuttle runs alone and another 20 effectively stuck waiting for Yanbu operations to return.

That is why the price signal moved from crude alone into freight. Reuters said the daily time-charter rate for a VLCC carrying Middle East crude to China hit a record $1.27 million on Monday, based on LSEG data. If you divide that by a 2 million-barrel cargo, one day of ship time is already roughly 64 cents per barrel before fuel, port costs, insurance, waiting time and the operational mess are counted. That is not a full delivered-cost calculation. It is just enough arithmetic to show why the queue matters.

This is also why a barrel flowing through Hormuz today is not the same economic object as a barrel moving through a normal peacetime loading program. The cargo may exist. The tanker may exist. The buyer may exist. But if the transfer point is saturated, the market has to buy time. Time is now being quoted at seven figures a day.

The War Did Not Remove The Oil Shock; It Repriced The Logistics

Earlier in the year, the market’s working assumption was that alternate routes, emergency stocks and demand weakness could keep the shock from becoming a full-blown physical shortage. That was not wrong. It was incomplete. Backup systems do not provide infinite capacity. They provide a bridge until the bridge itself starts carrying too much traffic.

The International Energy Agency’s September oil market report shows why this matters beyond one anchorage off Oman. The IEA said global observed oil inventories fell by another 95 million barrels in August, taking cumulative draws since February to 507 million barrels, or 2.8 million barrels per day on average. It also said oil-on-water volumes declined by 65 million barrels as tanker traffic out of the Middle East came under renewed attacks. Those are not vibes. Those are barrels disappearing from the cushion that lets consumers pretend shipping risk is someone else’s problem. See the IEA’s September 2026 Oil Market Report.

The United States is not magically outside this system. The Energy Information Administration’s latest weekly table available before the September 30 release showed total U.S. petroleum stocks including the Strategic Petroleum Reserve at 1,535.9 million barrels for the week ending September 18, down 152.0 million barrels from a year earlier. Total stocks excluding the SPR were 1,251.4 million barrels, down 30.6 million barrels year over year, according to EIA weekly petroleum data. That does not mean the country is out of fuel. It means there is less room to absorb freight shocks without prices noticing.

Diesel Is Where The Queue Gets Personal

Crude oil headlines are dramatic, but diesel is where ordinary people meet the problem. Diesel moves trucks, farm equipment, construction machines, rail freight, delivery fleets and a lot of backup power. When crude logistics get inefficient and refinery feedstock arrives late or expensive, diesel does not politely stay out of it.

The IEA said refined product and LPG exports from Gulf countries remained nearly 60%, or 3.7 million barrels per day, below February levels in its September report. It also said Gulf diesel and gasoil net exports averaged 390,000 barrels per day in August, just over a quarter of pre-war levels, while disruptions to Russia’s refining system compounded the loss. That is the part that turns a maritime bottleneck into a grocery-store and harvest-season issue.

For a trucking company, the problem is not whether Brent settled at a neat round number. The problem is whether diesel suppliers can replace missing molecules without bidding up cargoes from farther away. For a farmer, the problem is whether fuel, fertilizer logistics and hauling costs all tighten at the same time. For a refinery, the problem is whether crude arrives on a schedule that lets the plant run efficiently. You do not need a dramatic shortage to raise costs. A slow, expensive supply chain will do the job just fine.

Do Not Confuse Movement With Normal

There is a tempting mistake here: if tankers are moving, then the crisis must be easing. That is how people get fooled by workarounds. A workaround can move a lot of oil and still be bad news.

Ship-to-ship transfer is normal in parts of the oil trade, but scale matters. When it becomes the main release valve for a major exporter under wartime routing pressure, the operation starts absorbing ships that would otherwise be making regular long-haul deliveries. Every extra day waiting for a transfer is a day that tanker is not completing another voyage. Every extra shuttle leg makes the same barrel require more steel, more crews, more scheduling and more risk.

This is the follow-through to the earlier tanker problem discussed in the Hormuz shuttle tanker freight shock. The market did not run out of imagination. It found another route. Then that route started charging rent.

That is the dry little joke inside energy security planning. Redundancy works until everyone needs the same redundancy at the same time.

What To Watch Next

The useful watchlist is short.

  • Transfer capacity off Oman: If ship-to-ship operations stay at their limit, tanker rates can remain high even if crude supply headlines look better.
  • Yanbu and the East-West route: A sustained return of Red Sea exports would reduce pressure on Sohar. Another disruption would do the opposite.
  • VLCC day rates: The $1.27 million figure matters because it prices scarcity in ships, not just scarcity in crude.
  • Diesel and gasoil exports: Crude can move while product markets stay tight. That is the inflation channel most people actually feel.
  • Inventory reports: Weekly U.S. petroleum stocks and monthly IEA inventory data show whether the system is rebuilding cushion or still spending it.

The right conclusion is not panic. Panic is usually expensive and badly timed. The right conclusion is that the oil market’s emergency plumbing is now part of the price. If you buy fuel, ship goods, farm land, run equipment or depend on diesel-fed supply chains, the Sohar queue is not some distant maritime footnote. It is one more line item sneaking into the bill.
