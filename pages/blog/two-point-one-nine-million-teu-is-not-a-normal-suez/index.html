Two Point One Nine Million TEU Is Not A Normal Suez

The Suez Canal is getting some container ships back. That does not mean the Red Sea shipping problem is over; it means importers now get a cheaper bill with a risk surcharge stapled to it.

The Number That Looks Like Relief

The useful number today is 2.19 million TEU. Lloyd’s List Intelligence said containership traffic through Suez reached that level in September 2026, up 40% month over month, as more carriers switched back from the longer Cape of Good Hope route. That sounds like a reopening. It is not quite that tidy, because the same brief says September 2023 traffic, before the Houthi Red Sea campaign, was 5.9 million TEU. In plain English: Suez is recovering, but it is still not close to normal.

That gap is the story. A canal that is partly back changes freight pricing quickly. It releases effective capacity, shortens voyages, frees up ships, and weakens the case for sky-high spot rates on Asia-Europe lanes. But a canal that is only partly back also keeps insurers, carriers and shippers pricing in the possibility that the shortcut stops being a shortcut again. Wonderful. The supply chain found a dimmer switch instead of an on-off switch.

The current snapshot from Lloyd’s List Intelligence is especially useful because it does not pretend the recovery is uniform. It says the Red Sea return is gathering pace but uneven, with gains in some segments and slower recovery in others. It also reported that a Chinese-operated containership was fired upon off Yemen on October 7, with all crew safe. That is not the sentence you want in the brochure for a fully normalized trade lane.

The bottom line: September’s Suez rebound is real, but still far below normal. Freight rates are easing in places because capacity is returning, while security risk keeps the invoice unstable.

Freight Rates Are Moving Like The Route Is Half Safe

Drewry’s latest World Container Index gives the market version of the same argument. On October 15, Drewry said its composite index rose 3% to $4,485 per 40-foot container, after three weekly declines. Rates from Shanghai to Rotterdam rose 6% to $3,522, and Shanghai to Genoa rose 7% to $3,945. That is not a collapse in shipping costs. It is a market bouncing between returned capacity and carrier attempts to hold pricing.

The important detail is underneath the headline. Drewry said fewer blank sailings and the resumption of Suez Canal transits are adding capacity to the Asia-Europe route, while carriers are trying to push higher FAK rates of $4,500 to $4,800 per 40-foot container effective October 19. Drewry’s wording is dry, but the meaning is direct: carriers want more money; extra capacity makes that harder; geopolitical risk keeps the argument alive. See the Drewry World Container Index assessment for the current lane numbers.

For an importer, this is not academic. If you buy furniture, electronics, parts, packaging, appliances, apparel, or seasonal inventory from Asia into Europe, the Red Sea decision now sits inside your landed cost. A few hundred dollars per container can be absorbed on some cargo. On lower-margin goods, bulky items, or small importers with weak bargaining power, it lands in the quote, the delivery date, or the retail price. Sometimes all three, because logistics departments also enjoy multitasking misery.

Why More Suez Traffic Can Lower Prices Without Fixing Risk

Container freight is not priced only by distance. It is priced by capacity, reliability, vessel utilization, insurance, bunker fuel, port timing and the carrier’s confidence that today’s route will still be usable next week. The Cape of Good Hope detour burns more fuel and ties up ships for longer. When vessels come back through Suez, the same global fleet can move more boxes in the same amount of calendar time. That tends to push spot rates lower, especially when demand is not strong enough to soak up the added capacity.

But security risk does not disappear just because some ships return. It becomes more selective. Carriers may route some services through Suez and keep others around the Cape. They may charge surcharges by lane, customer, commodity, insurance exposure, vessel type or destination. They may announce a return, then pause it after an incident. That uncertainty is expensive even when the posted freight rate is falling.

This is why a simple “Suez is back” headline is too neat. September’s 2.19 million TEU figure matters because it proves carriers are testing and using the route again. The comparison with 5.9 million TEU in September 2023 matters because it shows how much traffic still has not come back. The market is no longer paying only for a full detour. It is paying for optionality, caution and the administrative joy of making a shipping plan that can be wrong by Friday.

The Consumer Price Link Is Boring, Which Is Why It Matters

Ocean freight is usually a small share of the shelf price of a finished consumer product. That leads people to dismiss it. They should not. Freight spikes do not need to double the price of a toaster to matter. They can squeeze importer margins, delay promotions, reduce inventory variety, make small retailers carry less stock, or push costs into boring line items that nobody notices until the replacement part is out of stock.

The effect is sharper for heavy, bulky, low-margin goods. A container full of high-value electronics can absorb a freight swing more easily than a container full of furniture, tires, ceramic tile, canned goods, basic apparel, or low-cost household items. The cheaper the item per cubic foot, the louder the freight bill gets. That is why shipping shocks often show up unevenly: one category looks fine, another quietly gets uglier.

The timing also matters. Retailers and manufacturers are not just buying transportation for today. They are booking inventory around holidays, factory schedules, agricultural inputs, spare parts and production runs. A partly reopened Suez route can tempt companies to trim lead times again. A renewed attack can punish that decision. If you run procurement, this is where the spreadsheet starts developing opinions.

Energy Markets Still Sit Under The Shipping Story

This is an Energy post, not because containers are crude oil, but because shipping risk and fuel costs now keep feeding each other. Longer routes burn more bunker fuel. War-risk premiums raise delivered costs. Tanker and container risk share the same regional map even when the cargoes are different. A Red Sea disruption can affect boxes, crude, refined products, fertilizers and food inputs through different channels at the same time.

That is why this Suez rebound should be read alongside other chokepoint stories, not instead of them. Notavello has already covered how a vessel incident can turn into an import-cost problem in the container-shipping side of the Hormuz shock. The Suez version is less cinematic today, but more useful: the route is not closed, not normal, and not cheap enough to ignore.

The market is also showing the difference between a physical shortage and a risk premium. There are ships. There is cargo. There is a route. The problem is that every participant has to price the chance that the route becomes slower, costlier or politically unusable with very little warning. That is enough to keep volatility in the invoice even when headline capacity improves.

What To Watch Next

The next signal is not one magic oil price or one dramatic military headline. It is whether Suez traffic keeps rising without a matching jump in incidents, insurance costs or emergency surcharges. If the September rebound continues through October and November, spot rates on Asia-Europe lanes should face more pressure from added effective capacity. If attacks increase, carriers can pull back quickly, and the Cape detour becomes the expensive default again.

Three practical numbers are worth watching:

  • Suez container volume: whether traffic keeps moving toward the pre-crisis baseline or stalls far below it.
  • Asia-Europe spot rates: especially Shanghai-Rotterdam and Shanghai-Genoa, because they show whether returning Suez capacity is actually cutting shipper costs.
  • War-risk and route surcharges: because base freight can fall while the all-in invoice stays stubborn.

For ordinary buyers, this means the Red Sea problem is no longer just a crisis headline. It is a quiet cost embedded in imported goods, delivery promises and inventory decisions. The canal is open enough to matter. It is not normal enough to relax. That is the awkward middle, and supply chains love awkward middles about as much as they love surprise invoices.
