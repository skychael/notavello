EIA Reports $5.695 Diesel As Freight Volumes Stay Lower

Diesel finally moved the right way. That does not mean freight, heating oil, or grocery logistics got cheap again.

Diesel Fell, But Not Enough To Reset Freight

The verified development is simple: the U.S. Energy Information Administration put the national average on-highway diesel price at $5.695 a gallon for November 23, 2026, down 24.2 cents from the previous week. That is real relief, not a rounding error. It is also still $1.864 above the same week last year, according to EIA’s Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update.

That is why this is not a victory-lap story. Diesel has come down from the September record, but the economy that uses diesel does not get to pretend September never happened. Truckers bought fuel at the peak. Carriers priced loads during the peak. Shippers delayed decisions during the peak. Now prices are lower, but the margin damage is still sitting on balance sheets like an unwelcome houseguest.

The Associated Press reported this week that high diesel prices and thin profit margins are straining small U.S. trucking companies. One useful number from the report: at October’s average diesel price, a tractor-trailer getting six miles per gallon would use $521 of fuel for a 500-mile day, compared with $310 in February, based on DAT calculations cited by AP. Freight volumes were also lower than a year earlier for all three major trailer types. That is the part that matters for ordinary people: weak volumes do not automatically mean cheap delivery when the fuel line is still this expensive. The freight market can be slow and costly at the same time. Charming system, really.

The bottom line: EIA’s latest diesel price fell 24.2 cents in a week to $5.695. But distillate stocks are still below last year, and small trucking firms are still operating in a fuel market built for stress.

The Inventory Cushion Is Still Thin

The price drop came alongside a modest build in the fuel pool that matters most for trucking, farming, heating oil, rail, construction, and backup power. In the Weekly Petroleum Status Report for the week ending November 20, 2026, EIA put U.S. distillate fuel oil stocks at 105.6 million barrels, up from 104.9 million a week earlier. That is a 700,000-barrel build.

But the same EIA table shows distillate inventories were 112.2 million barrels a year earlier. So the market gained a little breathing room week to week while still sitting 6.6 million barrels below last year. East Coast distillate stocks were 24.2 million barrels, down from 27.9 million a year earlier. New England had 3.5 million barrels, compared with 3.8 million last year and 5.8 million two years earlier.

Those regional numbers matter because distillates are not just one thing. They cover diesel, heating oil, and related middle distillate fuels. A tight distillate market can hit a refrigerated truck, a snowplow contractor, a grain hauler, a home heating tank, and a grocery distribution center through different doors. Same house fire, multiple entrances.

Why Trucking Feels This Before Shoppers Do

Retail gasoline gets the headlines because drivers see it on every corner sign. Diesel works differently. You may never buy a gallon of diesel directly, but you rent a little bit of it every time food, appliances, building materials, medicine, parcels, or farm inputs move across the country.

Small carriers feel the squeeze first because they have less room to average out bad weeks. A large fleet can hedge, negotiate, delay equipment purchases, push fuel surcharges, or move freight across a wider network. A small carrier with a few trucks mostly has fewer tricks. It can drive, park, borrow, or quit. None of those options makes groceries cheaper.

AP’s trucking report is important because it connects the fuel price to behavior in the real freight market, not just a chart on a commodities screen. When a 500-mile day costs hundreds more in fuel than it did before the war shock, a load that looked marginal becomes bad business. If enough marginal loads stop moving, capacity gets weird: some lanes are oversupplied, others get tight, and the customer hears a familiar sentence: delivery will cost more than expected.

This is also why the recent Notavello fuel coverage has focused on diesel rather than just crude. Crude is the big upstream signal, but diesel is where the oil shock gets practical. If you want the longer version of that logic, see why diesel is where the oil shock stops being abstract.

Oil Prices Are Lower Than The Panic, Not Low

Crude prices are not back to normal either. AP reported early Thursday, November 26, 2026, that Brent crude was trading at $90.79 a barrel and benchmark U.S. crude at $84.10, with Middle East tensions still in the market even as global supply continued recovering. AP also noted Brent had been around $72 a barrel before the war began in late February.

That gap is one reason diesel has not simply snapped back. Refiners can turn crude into diesel, gasoline, jet fuel, and other products, but they cannot magically produce the exact barrel everyone wants in the exact region where it is needed. If shipping risk, refinery maintenance, regional stock levels, and winter demand all lean the wrong way at once, a lower crude price may soften the hit without removing it.

There is also the Strait of Hormuz problem. AP reported Thursday that incidents involving commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters have increased recently, and that October had the most reported attacks on commercial shipping since March, citing UKMTO. Before the war, the waterway handled about a fifth of the world’s traded oil and natural gas, according to AP’s regional reporting. That does not mean every barrel is stopped today. It does mean markets keep adding a risk premium because ships, insurers, crews, refiners, and buyers are not paid to be optimistic for free.

The Weekly Numbers To Watch Next

For the next report, do not just look at whether diesel is up or down. Watch the shape of the market. A falling pump number with rebuilding inventories is useful relief. A falling pump number with shrinking stocks is a warning that demand weakness, refinery choices, or temporary discounts are doing more work than supply recovery.

The clean read today is this: the diesel price drop is welcome, measurable, and useful. It is not enough to call the freight problem over. A $5.695 national diesel average is still an expensive operating environment, especially with distillate stocks below last year and trucking volumes already soft.

What This Means For Ordinary Prices

You should not expect every grocery item, package delivery fee, contractor estimate, or heating oil quote to fall just because diesel dropped for one week. Fuel costs move through supply chains unevenly. Some businesses adjust surcharges quickly. Some wait for monthly contracts. Some already absorbed losses and will use the relief to survive rather than cut prices. Annoying, but not mysterious.

The better expectation is slower pressure, not instant reversal. If diesel keeps falling and inventories rebuild into December, freight inflation should have less fuel behind it. If distillate stocks stall near current levels or shipping risk worsens again, the market can tighten quickly. Diesel has already shown it can move violently in this war cycle.

So today’s number is good news with an asterisk the size of a tanker. The U.S. has a little more distillate in storage, diesel is cheaper than it was a week ago, and crude has cooled from the worst panic levels. But the system is still running on a thin cushion. That is not a shortage claim. It is what the latest EIA inventory and price data say plainly enough.
