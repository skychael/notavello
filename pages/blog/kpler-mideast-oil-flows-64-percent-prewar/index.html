Kpler Says Mideast Oil Flows Fell to 64% of Prewar Levels

Kpler’s preliminary November data shows Middle East oil still moving, but through a much thinner system. That is not a shutdown; it is a working supply chain with fewer easy barrels and more ways to break.

Kpler’s November Flow Drop Is the Development

The important number today is not a missile count or another round of diplomatic throat-clearing. It is 15 million barrels a day. According to Kpler data reported by the Associated Press, oil moving out of the Middle East by all available routes in November amounted to about 15 million barrels per day, or roughly 64% of prewar flows. That was down from 97% of prewar supply in October.

The Strait of Hormuz itself looked weaker than the broader route system. Kpler’s preliminary figures through Monday put crude flows through the strait at 8.5 million barrels per day, about 50% of prewar levels. The broader 15 million barrel figure includes the U.S.-protected tanker route near Oman and pipelines that bypass the strait. In other words, the headline is not that Gulf oil stopped. The headline is that the workaround is doing a lot of work, and that is rarely free.

The Associated Press report also notes the obvious caveat: preliminary tanker-tracking numbers can rise as voyages are confirmed and cargoes are classified. Fine. Oil data is not a Fitbit step count. But the direction matters because it reverses the comforting October story, when flows had crawled back close to normal despite the war-risk cloud. You can read the AP’s underlying report here: AP on November Middle East oil flows and Kpler data.

The bottom line: The Hormuz problem is no longer a yes-or-no closure story. Kpler’s 64% flow figure means the market has barrels, but fewer easy barrels.

Why 15 Million Barrels a Day Still Matters

A 15 million barrel-per-day flow is enormous in ordinary language and uncomfortable in oil-market language. The Strait of Hormuz is not a scenic inconvenience. The International Energy Agency says almost 20 million barrels per day of oil moved through the strait in 2025, about a quarter of world seaborne oil trade. The same IEA factsheet says alternative export capacity is limited, with an estimated 3.5 million to 5.5 million barrels per day of available crude-routing capacity outside the strait. That is useful. It is not magic.

The U.S. Energy Information Administration gives a similar scale check. In the first half of 2025, total oil flows through Hormuz averaged 20.9 million barrels per day, equal to about 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption and one-quarter of maritime-traded oil. The EIA also notes that Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Iran have some pipeline options, but those alternatives can move only part of the usual Hormuz volume. The chokepoint is not called a chokepoint because analysts ran out of nautical vocabulary.

This is why a fall to 64% of prewar regional flows is not just an energy desk curiosity. It means refiners, traders and governments have to decide which cargoes get priority, which voyages need escorts, which insurers are willing to underwrite the trip, and which buyers pay more for certainty. Those costs do not always appear at the pump the next morning. They often show up first in freight contracts, diesel margins, refinery scheduling, airline fuel hedges and farm-input delivery timing.

The U.S. Cushion Improved in One Place and Thinned in Another

The latest U.S. inventory data argues against panic, but not against pressure. In the EIA’s Weekly Petroleum Status Report released November 18, commercial crude oil inventories rose by about 3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million barrels for the week ending November 13. Distillate fuel oil stocks, the bucket that includes diesel and heating oil, rose by 1.6 million barrels to 104.9 million barrels. That is the part of the report traders can point to and say, reasonably, that the U.S. system is not out of fuel.

The less comfortable part is the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. The same EIA report put the SPR at 276.4 million barrels, down 3.2 million barrels from the prior week and 32.7% below the year-earlier level. The country still has an emergency reserve. It just has less slack than it had before the war drew repeated emergency responses out of the system. The weekly EIA source is here: EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report.

That combination is exactly why the market can look calm on one screen and fragile on another. Commercial crude can build while emergency reserves fall. Diesel stocks can rise for a week while still sitting below more comfortable seasonal levels. A single weekly build is welcome. It is not a force field.

Why Prices Can Fall While Risk Rises

Oil prices do not move only on danger. They move on supply, demand, inventories, currency, refinery maintenance, freight, positioning and the collective mood of people who spend too much time watching blinking terminals. That is why prices can soften even while Hormuz remains ugly.

The IEA’s November Oil Market Report says its supply-and-demand balances suggest the global oil market will return to surplus in the fourth quarter of 2026, with a larger build projected in 2027. That matters. If demand is weak enough and non-Gulf supply is strong enough, prices can fall even when a key route is damaged. The IEA report is here: IEA Oil Market Report for November 2026.

But a surplus is not the same thing as a cheap, smooth logistics chain. A surplus says barrels exist somewhere. It does not say the right grade is in the right port, the tanker is willing to sail, the refinery can run it, or the resulting diesel is in the region that needs it. That distinction is where ordinary people get dragged into an oil story they did not ask to follow.

If Gulf barrels arrive late, take longer routes, or require higher war-risk cover, the friction can sit inside product prices even when crude benchmarks wobble lower. Diesel is especially exposed because it is the fuel of trucks, ships, construction equipment, farm machinery and winter heating in parts of the Northeast. Gasoline gets the headlines because drivers see it on signs. Diesel does more of the lifting.

Who Feels the 64% Flow Signal First

The first consequence is not necessarily a gas-station shortage. That would be the dramatic version, and dramatic versions are often late. The more likely early consequence is uneven relief: some prices ease, some surcharges stick, and some buyers discover that the cheap barrel is not the barrel they can actually get.

  • Truckers and shippers: Diesel exposure stays central. Even small moves in diesel matter when a carrier burns thousands of gallons across a fleet. Fuel surcharges do not politely vanish because crude prices had a decent week.
  • Farmers: Fuel and fertilizer logistics both matter. Nitrogen fertilizer depends heavily on natural gas and global trade routes, and farm operations depend on diesel for hauling, drying, planting and harvest work. If shipping risk keeps freight expensive, crop-input relief can be delayed even when commodity charts look calmer.
  • Refiners: Refineries need specific crude grades, not just any barrel with a passport. Disrupted Gulf flows can force substitution, change margins and complicate product yields.
  • Households: The pressure arrives through gasoline, heating oil, airfare, groceries and delivered goods. Not all at once. That would at least be tidy. Instead it leaks through invoices.

This is also why the U.S. distillate line matters more than the crude headline for many households. Notavello’s earlier check on diesel inventories made the same point from the retail side: diesel relief can be real while stocks remain tight.

What to Watch Next

The cleanest test is whether Kpler’s preliminary November numbers are revised materially higher as late cargo confirmations arrive. If the 15 million barrel-per-day figure holds near that level, the October recovery looks more like a temporary patch than a new normal. If it moves sharply higher, then the market may conclude that escorts, rerouting and pipeline use are still enough to keep the system limping forward.

The second test is the U.S. weekly inventory pattern. One commercial crude build does not settle the issue. Watch whether distillate stocks keep rising, whether the SPR keeps falling, and whether refinery utilization stays high without creating new product shortages. The worst mix for households would be simple: fragile Gulf flows, weak emergency reserves, and stubbornly tight diesel.

The third test is freight behavior. If charter rates and insurance premiums stay elevated even while crude benchmarks soften, that tells you the market is pricing route risk separately from barrel availability. That is the version consumers tend to notice later, when the fuel line is buried inside a grocery receipt, airline ticket or farm supply quote.

So no, this is not a generic war recap. The measurable development is narrower: Kpler’s November flow estimate says the Middle East oil system is moving only about 64% of prewar volumes across all available routes. That is enough to avoid the most dramatic shortage story today. It is also enough to keep the fuel-risk premium alive, which is annoying, expensive and very much the point.
