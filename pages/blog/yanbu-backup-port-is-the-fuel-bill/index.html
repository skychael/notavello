Yanbu Is The Backup Port On Your Fuel Bill

Saudi Arabia’s backup route around Hormuz is no longer just a clever map line. The Red Sea end of that route, Yanbu, is now part of the fuel-price problem.

The Backup Port Is Now In The Story

The current oil shock is not only about whether tankers can squeeze through the Strait of Hormuz. The more useful question is what happens when the detour also becomes dangerous.

On September 19, Saudi Arabia said Houthi forces tried to attack civilian infrastructure in Yanbu, the Red Sea port city where Saudi Arabia’s East-West Pipeline ends. The Saudi-led coalition said the attacks were thwarted, and the Associated Press reported that there were no confirmed reports of damage from the Riyadh missile incident tied to the same wave of attacks. The Houthis claimed they targeted Aramco facilities in Yanbu, but AP said it could not independently verify those claims and Aramco did not respond to a request for comment. That last sentence matters. In an oil panic, unverified fire claims travel faster than crude. AP’s report on the September 19 attacks is the clean version: attempted attack, strategically important location, no verified damage publicly confirmed.

Yanbu matters because it is not a random dot on the Red Sea. It is the loading outlet for the pipeline designed to move Saudi crude from the Persian Gulf side of the country to the Red Sea side, avoiding Hormuz. If Hormuz is the front door and the Red Sea route is the back door, Yanbu is the handle. You do not have to blow up the whole house to make the door less useful.

The bottom line: The market is not pricing a clean Hormuz closure anymore. It is pricing a messy system where every workaround has its own target, delay and surcharge.

This Is Why A Port Story Becomes A Pump Story

Saudi Arabia’s East-West Pipeline had already moved from background infrastructure to front-page infrastructure. AP reported earlier in the week that the pipeline was closed after an attack blamed by Saudi Arabia on drones from Iranian-backed militias in Iraq, and that two regional officials said repairs could take three to five weeks. The same report said the line is about 1,200 kilometers long and carries crude westward to Yanbu, where it can be loaded onto tankers for export. AP’s pipeline report put the repair window at weeks, not hours.

That is the ugly part of energy security. The bypass route is not magic. It is steel, pumps, ports, crews, insurers, ship schedules and weather windows. Hit one major pumping facility and the whole workaround loses capacity. Threaten the outlet port and shippers start asking what premium they need to load there. Threaten the sea lane after loading and the premium gets another premium, because apparently one surcharge was lonely.

This is also why the Yanbu attempt is different from a routine battlefield update. It sits directly on the path between crude production and export revenue. When that path gets less reliable, the cost does not politely stay in Saudi Arabia. It flows into crude benchmarks, diesel cracks, freight rates, airline fuel bills and eventually grocery shelves.

Notavello has already covered how the Saudi bypass became fragile when the East-West pipeline became the target. The new point is narrower and nastier: even if repairs restore some pipeline flow, the Red Sea export end now carries its own risk signal.

EIA Data Shows The Workarounds Were Already Carrying Too Much Weight

The Energy Information Administration’s September Short-Term Energy Outlook made the rerouting problem measurable. EIA estimated that oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz fell from 21.6 million barrels per day in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 4.9 million barrels per day in the second quarter of 2026. Over the same period, flows through Bab el-Mandeb rose from 5.4 million barrels per day to 8.1 million barrels per day. In plain English: less oil through Hormuz meant more stress on the Red Sea corridor. EIA’s global energy security data gives the table, not the vibes.

EIA also said in its September market review that attacks on Saudi Arabia’s oil exports through the Bab el-Mandeb area reduced exports departing from Yanbu, the Red Sea route used to bypass Hormuz. That is the sentence to underline. The official U.S. energy agency is not treating Yanbu as a theoretical risk. It is already part of the supply constraint.

The market can adapt to one blocked route. It can sometimes adapt to two. What it hates is uncertainty across the whole chain: Hormuz restricted, pipeline damaged, Red Sea export lanes threatened, ship-to-ship transfers doing extra work, and insurers staring at the map like it owes them money.

The U.S. Cushion Is Not Empty, But It Is Thin Where It Hurts

The latest U.S. weekly oil data do not show a country out of fuel. That would be the wrong claim. They show a country paying more because the cushion is less comfortable than normal.

For the week ending September 11, the EIA reported U.S. commercial crude stocks at 423.4 million barrels, slightly above a year earlier. That part is not the alarm bell. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve was 285.0 million barrels, down 29.8% from a year earlier. Distillate fuel oil stocks were 107.9 million barrels, up from the prior week but still 13.5% below the comparable week in 2025. Distillate is the diesel, heating oil and farm-fuel bucket. It is the bucket you notice when trucks, harvest equipment and heating demand start competing. EIA’s Weekly Petroleum Status Report released September 16 has the stock and price tables.

The price table is blunt. EIA listed WTI at Cushing at $101.27 per barrel for September 11, up from $92.69 a week earlier. U.S. regular gasoline averaged $4.319 per gallon on September 14, while on-highway diesel averaged $6.285 per gallon. Diesel was up 31.8 cents in a week and $2.546 above the comparable 2025 reading. That is not an abstract geopolitical premium. That is a trucking quote, a harvest bill and a delivery surcharge.

This is where the Yanbu story reaches ordinary people. You do not buy crude from Yanbu. You buy groceries that rode in a diesel truck, a plane ticket priced against jet fuel, heating oil in the Northeast, or inputs that move through a supply chain with fuel surcharges tucked into every invoice like glitter in carpet.

The Measurable Consequence Is Time, Not Just Barrels

Oil coverage often talks as if the only question is barrels per day. Barrels matter, obviously. But the current escalation is also about time: repair time, voyage time, inspection time, waiting time, and the extra days created when ships avoid one chokepoint only to crowd another.

If a pipeline is partly offline for weeks, exporters do not simply click a button labeled “use ships instead.” The ships need crews, insurance, loading windows, escorts in some cases, and a route that underwriters will tolerate. If Yanbu is viewed as riskier, the cost of using the workaround rises even when cargoes still move. That is how a non-catastrophic attempted attack can still matter.

The Red Sea route was supposed to relieve the Hormuz bottleneck. But EIA’s numbers show Bab el-Mandeb was already handling a much heavier load in the second quarter than it did before the war. Add attacks near Saudi infrastructure and the system becomes less like a bypass and more like a narrow bridge with traffic cones, an accident ahead and everyone pretending this is fine.

For industry, the immediate problem is planning. Refiners, airlines, farmers and freight companies can handle expensive fuel better than unpredictable fuel. A steady bad price can be budgeted. A jumpy bad price gets padded, hedged, marked up and passed along.

What To Watch Next

The useful signals now are concrete. First, watch whether Saudi officials or Aramco confirm damage, reduced throughput or resumed normal operations at the East-West Pipeline and Yanbu. No confirmation is not proof of no problem, but verified damage is what changes balances fastest.

Second, watch EIA’s next Weekly Petroleum Status Report on September 23. The key U.S. lines are distillate stocks, refinery utilization, crude imports, crude exports and retail diesel. If diesel stocks build while prices keep rising, the market is paying mostly for risk and logistics. If stocks fall again, the physical cushion is getting worse.

Third, watch the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb traffic data. The Hormuz story has trained everyone to stare east. The current stress is increasingly west of Saudi Arabia, where rerouted barrels meet a smaller, riskier export corridor.

The clean version is this: Yanbu was built into the backup plan. Now the backup plan has its own warning light. When that happens, fuel prices do not need a total shutdown to stay painful. They only need enough doubt to make every barrel, ship and gallon carry a little more fear in the price.
