AAA Puts Regular Gas At $3.5866 After EIA Reports A 5.2M-Barrel Build

The pump finally gave drivers a break before Thanksgiving. AAA’s national regular gasoline average fell to $3.5866 on November 25, while the latest EIA report showed gasoline inventories rising 5.2 million barrels.

The Pump Price Finally Moved Where Drivers Can Feel It

AAA listed the U.S. national average for regular gasoline at $3.5866 per gallon on November 25, 2026, down from $3.6149 the previous day, $3.7502 a week earlier, and $4.2892 a month earlier, according to AAA Fuel Prices. That is not cheap in any normal sense. It is, however, a very visible retreat from the October squeeze.

For a 15-gallon fill-up, the drop from one month ago is about $10.54. That is the sort of energy-market math people notice without needing a Bloomberg terminal, a refinery model, or a nephew who says he understands crude spreads.

The timing matters. This is Thanksgiving travel week, and gasoline is the fuel most households encounter directly. Diesel moves the groceries, jet fuel moves the planes, bunker fuel moves the imports, but regular unleaded is the one with the big glowing sign you stare at while wondering whether the gas station hot dog has legal immunity.

The bottom line: Gasoline relief is real, not imaginary. But diesel is still expensive, the SPR is still drawing down, and Middle East disruption risk has not politely left the room.

EIA’s Gasoline Build Gave The Market A Cushion

The latest complete EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report, released November 18 for the week ending November 13, showed total motor gasoline stocks at 215.9 million barrels. That was up 5.2 million barrels from the prior week and 8.6 million barrels above the same week in 2025.

That is the key development for ordinary drivers: inventories built at the same time retail gasoline prices were falling. A price decline with no stock cushion can be a temporary mood swing. A price decline with more barrels in storage has more substance behind it.

The same EIA table also showed commercial crude inventories, excluding the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, at 417.8 million barrels, up 3.4 million barrels on the week. Refineries were still taking in a heavy volume of crude, with crude inputs at 16.918 million barrels per day. In plain English: U.S. refiners were still working hard, and the gasoline tank got fuller.

There is one timing caveat. This post is published on November 25 before the usual 10:30 a.m. Eastern EIA weekly release for the following reporting week. So the verified inventory numbers here are the November 18 EIA release, not an unreleased report. Boring? Yes. Better than pretending to know data that is not out yet? Also yes.

This Is Relief, Not A Clean Reset

The gasoline number is the good news. The rest of the barrel is less cheerful.

EIA reported distillate fuel oil stocks at 104.9 million barrels in the same November 18 report. That was up 1.6 million barrels from the prior week, but still 6.2 million barrels below the year-earlier level. Distillates include diesel and heating oil, which means the weak spot for freight, farms, construction, heating customers, and a great many boring but essential supply chains has not vanished.

AAA’s November 25 diesel average was $5.7645 per gallon. That was down from $6.3474 a month earlier, but still far above the $3.7854 average a year earlier. Gasoline is where drivers feel the break first. Diesel is where the grocery shelf, parcel delivery route, and farm input ledger keep arguing.

That is why today’s gasoline move should not be read as a general victory parade for fuel costs. The earlier Notavello piece on diesel staying tight even after slipping below $6 still matters. A household can save on the holiday drive and still pay for expensive diesel indirectly through freight-heavy goods. Energy markets are generous like that.

The SPR Drawdown Is Still Part Of The Story

The EIA report also showed the Strategic Petroleum Reserve at 276.4 million barrels for the week ending November 13, down 3.2 million barrels from the prior week and down 134.6 million barrels from a year earlier. That is a large year-over-year draw, and it keeps the cushion question alive.

The point is not that the SPR is empty. It is not. The point is that the United States is getting near-term market relief while also using emergency inventory in a market still shaped by war risk, tanker risk, and refinery strain. That tradeoff can make sense in a crisis. It can also leave less room for the next ugly surprise.

EIA’s November Short-Term Energy Outlook said crude prices rose in October as falling global inventories, attacks on oil tankers in the Persian Gulf, constrained shipping capacity, and higher transportation costs tightened supply. It also forecast that global inventories would begin building in the fourth quarter of 2026, while warning that Middle East crude flows would remain susceptible to disruption.

That combination explains the current market pretty well: enough supply improvement to pull gasoline lower, enough geopolitical risk to keep everyone from relaxing like this is a normal November.

What To Watch Next

The next useful signals are not dramatic speeches. They are weekly inventory lines, refinery utilization, and whether distillate stocks keep rebuilding. A few things matter more than the noise:

  • Gasoline stocks: if they keep building, the retail price decline has a better chance of sticking through early winter.
  • Distillate stocks: if diesel and heating oil remain below last year, freight and heating costs can stay stubborn even while gasoline improves.
  • SPR changes: continuing draws may calm today’s market while shrinking tomorrow’s buffer.
  • Refinery runs: high runs help supply, but they also leave less slack if a refinery trips, a port slows, or crude logistics get awkward.
  • Middle East shipping risk: fewer headlines do not equal normal trade. Insurance, routing, crew risk, and tanker availability still matter.

For drivers, the practical conclusion is simple: Thanksgiving gasoline is less painful than it looked a month ago. For businesses, farmers, and shippers, the answer is less satisfying. Gasoline got relief first because gasoline inventories built hard. Diesel is improving, but it is still expensive enough to keep showing up in delivered prices.

So yes, the pump sign is finally moving in the right direction. Just do not confuse a better fill-up with a repaired fuel system. The market handed drivers a break. It did not hand anyone a warranty.
