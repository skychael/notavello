U.S. Fuel Builds Blunt a 20% Early-November Drop in Mideast Oil Exports

Mideast oil exports slipped hard in early November, but U.S. fuel inventories moved the other way. That is why the pump is getting a little cheaper while the supply chain is not exactly relaxing.

The Drop Was Real, But It Did Not Hit Alone

The useful number today is not another dramatic Strait of Hormuz headline. It is 13 million barrels per day. Rystad Energy told the Associated Press that oil flows from Mideast producers in the first two weeks of November fell by around 20% to roughly 13 million barrels per day, down from October levels that had been near the prewar range of 15 million to 16 million barrels per day. AP also reported that about 80% of the prewar volume is still being exported, much of it moving through Hormuz under U.S. protection, with some barrels using pipelines to the Red Sea and Gulf of Oman instead. AP’s November 20 report is a good antidote to two bad takes at once: no, the region is not flowing normally; also no, the world has not run out of oil by breakfast.

The reason this matters to you is that energy markets are now absorbing two opposite signals. On one side, attacks and rerouting are cutting into Mideast exports and keeping freight, insurance, and crew risk elevated. On the other side, U.S. petroleum inventories just built across the major consumer fuels. That second fact is why gasoline and diesel can soften even while the chokepoint story remains ugly. Markets are annoyingly capable of holding two thoughts at once.

The bottom line: The near-term consumer relief is real. The cushion behind it is thinner than the headline fuel builds make it look.

EIA’s Latest Inventory Report Gave Drivers Some Breathing Room

The U.S. Energy Information Administration’s latest Weekly Petroleum Status Report, for the week ending November 13, showed commercial crude stocks rising by 3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million barrels. Total motor gasoline inventories rose by 5.2 million barrels to 215.9 million barrels. Distillate fuel oil stocks, the bucket that includes diesel and heating oil, rose by 1.6 million barrels to 104.9 million barrels. Those are not tiny moves, especially in a week when traders were also staring at lower Mideast flows. The source report is available from the EIA weekly petroleum data page.

So the week gave consumers some relief, but not the kind of relief that lets anyone throw away the risk dashboard.

Pump Prices Are Falling From Painful Levels, Not Becoming Cheap

The price move is visible. EIA’s daily price page for November 20 listed Brent at $95.17 a barrel and WTI at $86.78 at the November 19 close. It also republished AAA retail averages showing regular gasoline at $3.69 a gallon and diesel at $5.90 a gallon. AAA’s own fuel-price table put the November 20 national averages at $3.6898 for regular gasoline and $5.8982 for diesel, with diesel still far above its year-ago average of $3.7929. EIA’s daily prices page and AAA’s fuel-price page show the same basic shape: falling lately, still expensive.

That distinction matters because falling from a spike is not the same thing as returning to normal. A trucking company, farm co-op, grocery distributor, school bus contractor, or heating-oil household does not budget off the direction of the line. It pays the invoice. Diesel below $6 is better than diesel above $6, obviously. It is still a nasty number when compared with last year, and it still works its way into food, freight, construction, and delivery costs.

Notavello flagged this same diesel problem in a recent diesel check: a better price can still sit on top of tight stocks. This week’s EIA data improve that picture, but only partly. Distillate inventories rose, yet remained 5.6% below the same week last year. That is not a shortage claim. It is a cushion claim. The cushion is not as plump as the weekly build makes it sound.

The SPR Number Is The Awkward Part

The uncomfortable line in the EIA report is the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. SPR crude stocks fell to 276.4 million barrels, down 3.2 million barrels on the week and 134.6 million barrels below the same week in 2025. That is a 32.7% year-over-year drop. The U.S. is getting some market stability from releases and exchanges, but those barrels are not magic. Once they leave the reserve, the reserve is smaller. Finance people call that a tradeoff. Everyone else calls it using the backup battery.

This is where the headline fuel builds can mislead. If you only look at commercial gasoline and diesel, the week looks comfortable. If you add the SPR draw and the still-low distillate level, the picture gets more conditional. The U.S. can soften a supply shock for a while. It can also turn emergency storage into current supply. What it cannot do is spend the same emergency barrel twice, unless someone in Washington has discovered alchemy and kept it off the procurement schedule.

For ordinary people, this shows up as volatility rather than a single clean price direction. Gasoline can fall before Thanksgiving while diesel stays historically high. Crude can soften while heating oil remains a winter worry. Export flows can drop in the Gulf while U.S. inventories build for a week. That is not contradiction. That is a stressed system with more than one valve.

What To Watch Next Week

The next useful test is whether this was a one-week inventory assist or the start of a more durable rebuild. Watch four numbers first: distillate stocks, refinery runs, commercial crude imports, and SPR changes. If distillate builds continue while diesel prices keep sliding, freight and farm fuel pressure should ease. If distillate stalls again, the sub-$6 diesel moment may look more like a pause than a reset.

  • Distillate inventories: The latest level, 104.9 million barrels, is better than the prior week but still below last year.
  • Gasoline stocks: The 5.2 million barrel build helps explain the recent drop at the pump.
  • SPR stocks: Another draw would keep the emergency-cushion question alive.
  • Mideast exports: The 13 million barrel-per-day early-November figure needs follow-up. A rebound would calm markets; another decline would test how much comfort U.S. inventories can provide.

The practical read is simple. Consumers are getting some price relief because U.S. tanks filled at the right moment. Industry is not getting an all-clear because the underlying export route is still unstable and the reserve cushion is still being drained. Cheaper than last month is good. Safe is a different word.
