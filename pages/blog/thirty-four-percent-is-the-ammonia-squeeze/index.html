Thirty Four Percent Is The Ammonia Squeeze

The newest farm-cost problem is not another dramatic tanker headline. It is ammonia from Trinidad, and the bill lands right in the corn budget.

The New Fertilizer Problem Is Not In Hormuz

The latest pressure point in U.S. fertilizer is not a tanker queue in the Strait of Hormuz. It is Trinidad and Tobago, a country that has long mattered more to American farms than most grocery shoppers would ever guess.

Reuters reported on November 2, 2026, that ammonia imports from Trinidad into the United States have dropped about 34% this year, adding to an already tight global market. Midwest ammonia prices climbed from about $600 per short ton in late June to about $800 by early October, and have since moved to around $875, according to the Reuters report citing industry analysts and CRU. That is not a rounding error. That is a nitrogen bill walking into fall application season wearing muddy boots. Reuters, via MarketScreener, detailed the Trinidad supply drop and Midwest ammonia price move.

This is the useful angle today because it is measurable and immediate. Farmers are pricing fertilizer for the 2027 crop while diesel is already expensive, crop margins are not exactly frothy, and the usual escape hatch — “someone else will ship it” — is not working cleanly. Energy shocks are very good at changing addresses. This one left the oil page and showed up on an input invoice.

The bottom line: U.S. ammonia imports from Trinidad are down about 34% this year, according to Reuters. That turns a gas-supply problem on one island into a nitrogen bill across the U.S. farm belt.

Why Trinidad Matters To A Corn Field In Illinois

Trinidad matters because ammonia is the base layer of nitrogen fertilizer. Nitrogen is what corn needs in large quantities, and ammonia is both a direct fertilizer and a feedstock for other nitrogen products. When ammonia supply tightens, the pressure does not politely stay in one product category. It can move into urea, UAN solutions, dealer inventories, delivery timing, and application choices.

The United States is not helpless here. It has domestic ammonia production, and North America also draws heavily from Canada. But Trinidad has historically been a major supplier. The U.S. Geological Survey’s 2026 nitrogen summary lists U.S. import sources for 2021 through 2024 as Canada at 49%, Trinidad and Tobago at 47%, and other sources at 4%. That does not mean every farm is directly buying Trinidad ammonia. It means losing a chunk of that flow matters to the whole price system. USGS’s 2026 nitrogen mineral commodity summary shows how concentrated U.S. ammonia import sources have been.

The underlying cause is wonderfully unglamorous: natural gas. Ammonia production depends heavily on gas, and Trinidad’s ammonia sector has been constrained by gas availability. So the chain looks like this: gas problems reduce ammonia exports; ammonia imports into the U.S. fall; dealers and farmers face higher prices; nitrogen plans get more expensive. Nobody needs a conspiracy board with red yarn. The supply chain is annoying enough on its own.

The Per-Acre Math Is Why This Becomes Food Inflation

Here is the farm-level math. Anhydrous ammonia is 82% nitrogen. One short ton contains 2,000 pounds, so a ton of anhydrous ammonia contains about 1,640 pounds of nitrogen. At $875 per ton, that nitrogen costs roughly 53 cents per pound before the rest of the real-world bill shows up. At $600 per ton, it was about 37 cents per pound.

If a corn budget uses 180 pounds of nitrogen per acre, the move from $600 to $875 per ton adds about $30 per acre. On 1,000 corn acres, that is roughly $30,000. That is before arguing about application, timing, equipment, financing, freight, or whether the local supplier even has the exact product the farm prefers. Agriculture has many ways to make a spreadsheet look tidy and then laugh at it in the yard.

USDA’s Illinois Production Cost Report is also flashing the same general problem. USDA’s Agricultural Marketing Service says the report tracks farm input costs, including synthetic fertilizer, with low, high, and average prices gathered from trade information. The late-October Illinois data put anhydrous ammonia near $981 per short ton and urea near $770 per short ton in average dealer asking prices, based on USDA-linked market reporting. USDA AMS describes the Illinois Production Cost Report and its fertilizer price collection.

That is why this is not just a farmer complaint. Corn feeds livestock, supports ethanol, affects sweeteners and starches, and moves through a food system that already has expensive diesel, expensive labor, and expensive credit. Fertilizer does not instantly become a grocery receipt, but it does become one eventually. The lag is not mercy. It is just accounting.

The War Still Matters, But Not As A Simple Shortage Story

The Iran war still sits behind much of the energy stress. Earlier disruptions through the Persian Gulf tightened global fertilizer, oil, diesel, and freight markets. But the Trinidad ammonia story is useful because it keeps the analysis honest. Not every painful price move comes from one dramatic chokepoint. Sometimes a global market is tight because several different problems stack on top of each other.

That distinction matters. Claiming “fertilizer shortage” every time a price jumps is lazy. The current evidence points to a tighter ammonia market, reduced Trinidad supply into the United States, elevated prices, and awkward timing for farmers. That is enough. You do not need to inflate it into apocalypse confetti.

It also explains why relief is hard. If the problem were only crude oil, governments could talk about emergency barrels, refinery runs, export policy, or fuel waivers. If the problem were only Hormuz, shippers could reroute, convoy, insure, or wait. With ammonia, the bottleneck includes gas supply, chemical production, import flows, fertilizer terminals, local dealer availability, and agronomic timing. Farmers cannot simply decide in February that October nitrogen logistics were a vibe.

This is the sequel to the fall ammonia bill, except the plot has moved from broad war risk to a very specific import channel.

Government Aid Can Miss The Actual Invoice

The politics are obvious because the calendar is obvious. Reuters noted that President Donald Trump announced an $8 billion financial support program last month aimed at offsetting diesel costs for farmers, while farm groups warned that fertilizer costs were eating into the value of that relief. Diesel matters. It runs harvest, trucking, drying, and fieldwork. But nitrogen is its own monster.

USDA’s farm income forecast already showed the broader squeeze before this latest Trinidad story. USDA’s Economic Research Service forecast total U.S. farm production expenses at $492.8 billion in 2026, up $21.2 billion from 2025. It expected fertilizer, lime, and soil conditioner expenses to rise by $5.3 billion, or 15.3%, and fuel and oil expenses to rise by $4.8 billion, or 28.8%. USDA ERS’s farm income forecast lays out the 2026 production-expense increase.

That is the policy headache. A diesel check does not buy ammonia. A fuel waiver does not restart gas-fed production in Trinidad. A press conference does not lower the nitrogen requirement of corn. Farmers may get some cash relief and still face a higher cost structure for next year’s crop. This is how emergency policy can be directionally correct and still feel half-useless at the counter.

What To Watch Next

The next useful signals are not speeches. Watch import volumes, dealer quotes, and whether ammonia prices keep pulling other nitrogen products higher. Watch natural gas availability in Trinidad. Watch whether U.S. domestic plants run smoothly enough to cover more of the gap. Watch fall application progress, because timing matters almost as much as price when weather closes the window.

For ordinary people, the takeaway is simpler: food inflation does not begin at the checkout lane. It starts months earlier with diesel, fertilizer, freight, interest, and weather. The grocery bill is the edited version. The farm input bill is the rough draft, and right now the rough draft has a Trinidad ammonia problem written all over it.

That is why 34% matters. It is not a headline number from a distant commodity desk. It is a missing import flow in a concentrated supply chain, arriving exactly when farmers need to lock down nitrogen for the next crop. Energy shocks do not have to look dramatic to be expensive. Sometimes they look like a fertilizer quote that got worse while nobody outside the farm belt was looking.
