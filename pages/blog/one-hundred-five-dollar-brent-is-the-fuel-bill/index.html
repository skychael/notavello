Energy October 13, 2026

One Hundred Five Dollar Brent Is The Fuel Bill

The useful number this week is not another map arrow over the Strait of Hormuz. It is EIA’s new $105 Brent forecast for the fourth quarter, because that is where tanker risk starts leaking into diesel, gasoline, heating oil and freight.

The Forecast Moved From Risk To Baseline

The Energy Information Administration’s October Short-Term Energy Outlook is the cleanest starting point because it puts a number on the problem. EIA now forecasts Brent crude oil to average $105 per barrel in the fourth quarter of 2026, which is $14 higher than its September outlook. That is not a fringe blog chart. That is the U.S. government’s baseline energy forecast after months of constrained Middle East flows, convoy routing, workarounds, ship-to-ship transfers, and inventory withdrawals. See EIA’s October outlook here: https://www.eia.gov/OUTLOOKS/STEO/.

The annoying part, for anyone hoping for a clean relief story, is that the forecast was already bounded by time. EIA says its model inputs were finalized on October 1, 2026. It includes the 40 million barrels of U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve exchanges announced September 29, but it does not account for additional crude or petroleum products from the G7 announcement on October 2. In plain English: the official forecast already sees expensive oil, and it still cannot fully price everything that happened after the cutoff.

That matters because this is not a generic war recap. The market has a fresh reference price. Reuters reported that on October 12 Brent settled at $106.65 a barrel and WTI settled at $93.74, after Saudi attacks and tanker incidents outweighed the prospect of more supply. The Reuters report is here: https://www.investing.com/news/commodities-news/oil-climbs-as-mideast-conflict-tanker-attacks-persist-4942900.

The bottom line: The Gulf risk premium is no longer just a trader’s mood. EIA raised its fourth-quarter Brent forecast to $105 a barrel, and the latest weekly petroleum table still shows thin distillate and SPR cushions.

The Tanker Problem Is A Price Problem

There is a lazy version of the story that says oil is either flowing or blocked. That is too neat, which is how it tells on itself. EIA’s October outlook assumes Middle East oil flows stay constrained through the fourth quarter, but also assumes exports improve through convoys, bypass routes and ship-to-ship transfers. So the issue is not a cartoon valve labeled open or shut. The issue is that every workaround has a cost.

Reuters cited UK Maritime Trade Operations reports that a tanker was struck by a drone near Oman’s Sohar area and another was damaged while transiting Hormuz. UKMTO’s live warning page also lists time-late reports and warnings for incidents in the Strait of Hormuz region: https://www.ukmto.org/. That does not mean every cargo stops. It means crews, insurers, charterers and refiners must price the route as hostile enough to deserve money.

You can see the result in the split between Brent and WTI. Brent is the global seaborne marker. WTI is landlocked into a different logistical reality, even when U.S. exports connect it to the world. When tankers, Gulf routes and alternative export channels get expensive or dangerous, Brent carries more of the pain. This is why a Strait of Hormuz story can show up in a trucking invoice in Pennsylvania or a heating-oil quote in Maine without any homeowner having the slightest interest in maritime security.

Diesel Is Still The Ugly Middle

Crude is the headline. Diesel is the part that makes the grocery bill, construction bid, farm harvest and delivery route less pleasant. EIA said September U.S. retail gasoline averaged $4.35 per gallon and retail diesel averaged $6.29 per gallon, driven by higher crude prices and rising crack spreads. It also expects retail diesel to average about $4.50 per gallon in 2027, which is not cheap fuel. It is just less dramatic fuel.

The reason diesel refuses to behave is that crude supply risk is colliding with distillate tightness. Distillates include diesel and heating oil. EIA said East Coast distillate inventories fell 32% below their five-year seasonal average in September and are expected to remain about 20% below the 2021–2025 average through the coming winter. That is the sort of sentence that sounds technical until you run a truck fleet or heat a house with oil.

If you want the refinery-side version of this problem, the earlier Notavello piece on diesel cracks as the price signal is the right companion read. The short version: when refiners can sell diesel at a fat premium to crude, the market is telling them it wants more middle distillates. When inventories are still low anyway, the market is also telling you that wanting more does not instantly create more.

The Inventory Cushion Is Not Generous

EIA’s weekly petroleum table for the week ending October 2, 2026 shows why emergency barrels and commercial inventories are not a magic wand. U.S. total crude stocks stood at 707.1 million barrels, down 120.1 million barrels from a year earlier. Commercial crude stocks excluding the SPR were 424.1 million barrels. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve was 283.0 million barrels, down 124.0 million barrels from a year earlier. The table is here: https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/supply/weekly/pdf/table1.pdf.

That does not mean the United States is out of oil. It means the cushion is thinner than it looks when people casually add commercial inventories and the SPR together and call it comfort. Commercial barrels are market barrels. SPR barrels are policy barrels. They can interact, but they are not the same thing. A barrel released through an exchange today also becomes a barrel owed back later. Free lunch remains unavailable, as usual.

The same table shows distillate fuel oil stocks at 105.1 million barrels, down 16.4 million barrels, or 13.5%, from a year earlier. Ultra-low sulfur distillate, the main diesel pool, was down 15.2% from a year earlier. That is the measurable reason diesel is the pressure point, not just a gloomy anecdote from a truck stop.

What Ordinary Buyers Should Watch Now

The practical question is not whether you can forecast every attack, escort route, pipeline repair and ministerial announcement. You cannot. The useful question is which numbers will tell you whether the pain is spreading.

  • Brent above the EIA baseline: If Brent spends time materially above the $105 fourth-quarter forecast, the market is pricing more than EIA’s October assumptions.
  • Diesel versus gasoline: A widening diesel premium means freight, farming, construction and heating oil users are still absorbing the sharper edge of the shock.
  • East Coast distillate inventories: This is the winter bill monitor. Low regional inventories matter more than a comforting national average.
  • SPR changes: A draw, exchange or loan can buy time. It does not erase the logistics problem if ships, ports, pipelines and refineries remain stressed.
  • UKMTO and maritime notices: One tanker incident can fade. Repeated incidents change behavior, and behavior changes freight rates.

For households, the first visible effect is usually heating oil, gasoline and delivery charges. For businesses, it shows up in fuel surcharges, supplier quotes, longer lead times and less willingness to guarantee transport prices. For farms, the same diesel pressure bleeds into harvest, drying, fertilizer logistics and spring planning. Nobody puts “Hormuz risk premium” as a line item on your invoice. That would be too honest.

The Takeaway Is Boring, Which Makes It Useful

The important development on October 13 is not that the Middle East is risky. Everyone knows that. The important development is that the risk has been translated into official price assumptions, current market settlements and visible inventory stress. EIA’s $105 Brent forecast is the baseline. Reuters’ $106.65 Brent settlement shows the market is already living there. EIA’s distillate and SPR numbers show the cushion underneath is not plush.

So the next fuel-price move does not require a full closure of the Strait of Hormuz. It can come from something less cinematic: another tanker hit, another convoy delay, another refinery snag, another week of weak distillate stocks, or another emergency-barrel announcement that sounds large until you compare it with daily consumption. The fuel bill is rarely dramatic at the cash register. It just keeps arriving.
