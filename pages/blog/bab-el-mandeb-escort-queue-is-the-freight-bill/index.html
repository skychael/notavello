Bab El-Mandeb Escort Queue Is The Freight Bill

The Red Sea problem is no longer just whether ships can pass. It is whether they can pass on time, insured, escorted and at a freight rate that does not quietly show up in diesel, food and factory costs.

The Chokepoint Did Not Close. That Is Not The Same As Safe.

The important development today is not a clean shutdown of Bab el-Mandeb. That would be easy to understand and impossible to miss. The more useful story is messier: ships are still moving, but the route is being repriced by escorts, detours, insurance caution and tanker scarcity.

That matters because Bab el-Mandeb is not a symbolic strip of water. It connects the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden and sits on the route between Europe and Asia through the Suez Canal. The Associated Press reported this week that around 12% of world trade, including about a fourth of global container traffic, passes through the narrows, and that the strait became a crucial alternative route for Saudi oil exports during the wider Iran war disruption around Hormuz. AP’s account of the Yemen escalation is worth reading because it frames the shipping problem correctly: this is no longer only a Red Sea story, and it is no longer only a Hormuz story.

The dry version is that the region now has overlapping chokepoint risk. The less dry version is that the world built a lot of cheap delivery promises on the assumption that tankers and container ships could use narrow waterways without needing a military appointment calendar. That assumption is taking dents.

The bottom line: Bab el-Mandeb is functioning, not normal. When tankers need escorts, when Saudi cargoes detour, and when VLCC rates hit historic highs, the bill does not stay at sea.

The New Price Signal Is The Escort Queue

Lloyd’s List Intelligence reported that Suez traffic remains elevated, with weekly transits averaging 287 over the four weeks through September 27, up from 281 in the preceding four weeks and 252 in June. Bab el-Mandeb transits were also above earlier levels, with a preliminary four-week average of 280, compared with 257 in January. On paper, that sounds like resilience. Ships are moving. Nobody needs to panic-buy canned beans. Excellent.

But the same Lloyd’s List Intelligence Red Sea brief also says commercial vessels are effectively queuing for escorts because Operation Aspides does not have enough warships to meet demand. That is the real signal. A waterway can be open and still be slow, expensive and operationally awkward.

An escort queue changes the cost of shipping in several ways at once. It can delay loading and discharge windows. It can force owners to pick between waiting, rerouting or accepting higher risk. It gives charterers a reason to pay up for tonnage that is already in the right place. It also makes schedules less reliable, which is exactly how a security problem becomes an inventory problem for refiners, traders, manufacturers and retailers.

This is the part people miss when they ask whether a route is open. Open is not the same as cheap. Open is not the same as predictable. Open with escorts is a different product.

Large Crude Tankers Are The Ones Voting With Their Rudders

The strongest measurable consequence is in crude tanker behavior. S&P Global reported that large tankers have steered away from Bab el-Mandeb as Houthi advances and attacks raised risks around the route. Its Commodities at Sea data showed no Very Large Crude Carriers transiting the strait after September 9, compared with 24 during August and 63 during July. That is not a vibes-based warning. That is tonnage leaving the room.

The same S&P Global shipping report said the Platts Global VLCC Index for non-scrubber, no-eco ships reached $970,635 per day on September 16, up from $519,041 per day on September 1 and $283,635 per day on July 20. That kind of move does not stay confined to shipowners’ spreadsheets. It changes crude delivered costs, refinery economics and eventually the margins behind diesel, jet fuel and heating oil.

There is also a route penalty. S&P Global reported that Saudi crude shipments to Asia using the Cape of Good Hope route made up 58% of Saudi-Asia flows in August, compared with 5% in July, and that the Cape route can add at least three weeks versus Bab el-Mandeb. Three weeks is not just a longer cruise. It is more fuel burned, more vessel days consumed, more working capital tied up and fewer available ships for the next cargo.

If you want the ordinary-person translation, it is this: the tanker market is spending more time and money to deliver the same barrel. That is how a distant strait becomes a line item in groceries, airfares, farm diesel and heating oil.

MARAD Is Telling Ships To Act Like The Threat Is Real

The U.S. Maritime Administration’s current advisory for the Red Sea, Bab el-Mandeb, Gulf of Aden, Arabian Sea and Somali Basin is not written like a tourism brochure. It tells U.S.-flagged commercial vessels to conduct pre-voyage risk assessments, incorporate protective measures into vessel security plans, monitor VHF Channel 16 and register with UKMTO before entering the Indian Ocean Voluntary Reporting Area. It also says crews not needed for safe navigation and operation should move to a safe space if a suspected UAV, missile or hostile small boat is observed.

That language matters because it confirms that the risk is operational, not just political. The advisory points vessels toward JMIC, UKMTO and maritime security guidance, and it specifically tells ships to include estimated arrival times at the Strait of Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb and Suez Canal when reporting. The MARAD advisory is not forecasting a fuel shortage. It is doing something more practical: telling mariners how to behave in a route system where missiles, drones, small boats and misidentification are now planning assumptions.

That is also why this is not a duplicate of the usual “war may disrupt oil” story. The measurable consequence is already here in voyage planning. Ships are reporting earlier, sailing differently, waiting for protection, avoiding certain legs or charging more to accept them. The market does not need a total closure to generate a surcharge. It only needs enough risk to make the next safe ship more expensive than the last one.

Why This Hits Diesel And Food Before It Looks Dramatic

The annoying thing about shipping shocks is that they rarely arrive as one clean retail price increase with a helpful label. They leak through the system. A tanker reroute adds days. A waiting period burns schedule slack. A higher charter rate raises the delivered cost of crude. A refiner paying more for crude or facing late arrivals protects margin where it can. Diesel wholesalers, trucking firms, farms and construction fleets then meet the new number at the rack or in contract renewals.

Diesel is especially exposed because it is not just a commuter fuel. It is the working fuel for freight, harvest, mining, backup generation, construction and a lot of boring activity that keeps shelves filled. When tanker costs rise, you may not notice it as “the Bab el-Mandeb fee.” You notice it as a stubborn freight quote, a delivery surcharge, a higher farm input bill or another excuse for food prices to refuse to calm down.

This is where the Red Sea links back to the broader energy chain Notavello has been tracking. The earlier Suez detour oil bottleneck was about route length and lost efficiency. Today’s version is sharper: some traffic is returning, but the return is conditional on escorts, insurance tolerance and tanker availability. That is better than a closed canal. It is also worse than a normal route.

The practical question for businesses is not “will the strait close tomorrow?” It is “how much delay and freight inflation can we absorb if the route stays half-normal for months?” That is a less dramatic question. It is also the one that lands on invoices.

What To Watch Next

Watch three numbers instead of chasing every battlefield update.

  • VLCC and Suezmax transit counts through Bab el-Mandeb. If large crude tankers keep avoiding the strait while smaller or non-oil traffic moves, the oil market is telling you the route is open but not trusted.
  • Escort availability and waiting times. Queues for naval protection are a hidden tax on schedules. They can matter as much as a headline attack because they change how many cargoes can move on time.
  • Delivered fuel spreads, not just Brent headlines. Brent above a round number gets attention, but diesel cracks, regional distillate inventories and tanker rates explain more of the pain that reaches farms, truckers and households.

The mistake is waiting for a cinematic closure. Chokepoints usually hurt the economy in duller ways first. A ship waits. A cargo detours. A charter rate jumps. A refinery pays more. A trucker pays more. Then someone asks why the grocery bill is still acting like the war is nearby.

Bab el-Mandeb has not disappeared from the map. That is the good news. The bad news is that it is being priced like a route that needs permission, protection and a backup plan. In shipping, that is already a bill.
