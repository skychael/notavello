Anhydrous Ammonia Is The Fall Farm Bill

The oil shock is not just a pump-price story. This week’s cleaner signal is anhydrous ammonia: the nitrogen bill farmers face before the next corn crop.

The Current Signal Is Not Crude. It Is Nitrogen.

The most useful energy-market number this week is not another dramatic Brent headline. It is DTN’s Sept. 30 retail fertilizer survey, which put anhydrous ammonia at an average of $977 per ton for the week of September 21-25. That was 6% higher than a month earlier and 25% higher than the same week in 2025.

That matters because fall is when many Corn Belt farms make or price nitrogen decisions for the next crop year. A crude spike gets television graphics. A fertilizer reset gets a spreadsheet tab, a call from the co-op, and possibly a less cheerful operating loan conversation. Very glamorous, agriculture.

The broader fertilizer board is not screaming shortage, and that distinction matters. DTN reported that all eight major fertilizers it tracks were above year-earlier levels, but anhydrous was the only one with a monthly move large enough to count as significant under DTN’s 5% threshold. In other words: this is not a generic panic story. It is a specific nitrogen-cost story.

The bottom line: Anhydrous ammonia averaged $977 per ton in late September, up 25% from a year earlier. That is not a grocery shortage. It is a quiet input-cost increase that shows up first in farm budgets.

The Per-Acre Math Is Annoyingly Real

Here is the rough farm-budget translation. DTN’s table shows anhydrous ammonia at $780 per ton in the week of September 22-26, 2025, and $977 per ton in the week of September 21-25, 2026. That is a $197-per-ton increase.

Anhydrous ammonia is 82% nitrogen. Supplying 180 to 200 pounds of nitrogen per acre takes roughly 220 to 244 pounds of product. At $197 more per ton, the year-over-year increase works out to about $22 to $24 more per acre for that nitrogen, before local basis, storage, application charges, timing, and dealer-specific pricing enter the room and start touching things.

That is not the whole cost of growing corn. It is one input line moving against the farmer before seed, land rent, repairs, interest, fuel, drying, crop insurance, and everything else gets its turn. But on 1,000 acres, even a $22-per-acre increase is a $22,000 problem. Nobody frames that and hangs it in the shop.

Why A Gulf War Shows Up In A Corn Budget

Nitrogen fertilizer is energy in a different outfit. The USDA Economic Research Service explains that ammonia is made by reacting nitrogen and hydrogen under heat and pressure, with natural gas playing a key role in the production process. Urea and other nitrogen fertilizers often start with ammonia. So when gas, shipping, and ammonia trade routes get stressed, fertilizer does not shrug.

The Middle East link is not decorative. In a September 24 statement, the FAO said the Gulf supplies 30% to 35% of global urea, 20% to 30% of ammonia, and 50% of sulfur exports. It also said 1.5 million to 3 million tonnes of monthly fertilizer trade had been delayed during the crisis.

That is the bridge from regional escalation to ordinary food costs. Not instantly. Not neatly. Not in one headline. The chain runs from gas and ammonia production, to shipping and port availability, to wholesale fertilizer, to local retail quotes, to spring acreage and input decisions, and finally to crop margins. If you want the earlier chokepoint version of this story, Notavello covered it in the fertilizer delay is the food price bill. Today’s update is the retail end: the price is still sitting in front of U.S. growers.

Anhydrous Is Still The Cheapest Nitrogen, Which Is The Catch

There is one awkward detail: even after the increase, anhydrous ammonia is still the cheapest nitrogen source in DTN’s comparison. DTN put anhydrous at $0.60 per pound of nitrogen, versus $0.73 for urea, $0.74 for UAN32, and $0.76 for UAN28.

That matters because farmers do not simply buy the lowest sticker price per ton. They buy timing, equipment compatibility, safety, labor availability, field conditions, and risk. Anhydrous is cost-efficient, but it requires specialized handling and application. UAN can be easier to manage and can be applied differently, but the nitrogen often costs more per pound. Urea is flexible but has its own loss and timing issues if not managed well.

So a higher anhydrous price does not automatically mean every farm switches products. It may mean growers sharpen the pencil on application rates, delay commitments, split applications, lean harder on agronomy advice, or accept a higher bill because the alternative is worse. Farming is full of choices like that. The options are real. The easy option is usually imaginary.

This Is A Margin Problem Before It Is A Food-Price Problem

The responsible way to read this is not: fertilizer is scarce and food prices must explode. The evidence does not support that. The responsible read is narrower and more useful: nitrogen costs have rebounded before fall application, and that makes farm margins more sensitive to crop prices, weather, and financing costs.

The University of Illinois farmdoc team estimated earlier this year that post-conflict fertilizer pricing could add more than $20 per acre to central Illinois corn fertilizer costs, depending on the nitrogen program. That estimate was made before the latest fall retail rebound, but it gives the right scale: tens of dollars per acre, not pennies, and not abstract geopolitics.

For ordinary people, that means the energy shock works through food slowly. Farmers may absorb some of it. Grain prices may absorb some of it. Retail food prices may never show a clean one-for-one line back to ammonia. But higher input costs reduce the cushion in the system, and thin cushions are how normal weather or logistics problems become expensive later.

What To Watch Next

The next useful signals are practical, not theatrical.

  • Retail nitrogen quotes: If anhydrous holds near $1,000 per ton into late fall, the cost problem becomes harder to dismiss as a short rebound.
  • Urea and UAN spreads: If anhydrous stays cheaper per pound of nitrogen, it remains attractive for equipped farms despite the higher headline price.
  • Gas and ammonia trade: Nitrogen prices do not move only with Henry Hub anymore; international gas, ammonia, and shipping matter.
  • USDA transportation data: USDA’s Fertilizer Transportation Dashboard update added anhydrous ammonia pipeline-rate data, which is exactly the kind of boring infrastructure number that becomes important when margins are tight.
  • Spring acreage decisions: If corn margins look weak and nitrogen remains expensive, acreage and fertilizer-rate decisions become the real market response.

That is the point. The Iran-war energy shock is not only measured in tanker queues and crude charts. Sometimes the receipt is a fall ammonia quote, a $24-per-acre increase, and a farmer deciding whether the next pass across the field still pays.
