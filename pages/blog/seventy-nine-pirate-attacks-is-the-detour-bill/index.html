Seventy Nine Pirate Attacks Is The Detour Bill

The Strait of Hormuz problem has a second chokepoint now. The route meant to work around Gulf disruption is picking up its own piracy bill.

The Detour Is Now A Target

The clean version of the oil-shipping map says trouble in the Strait of Hormuz can be softened by moving more barrels through alternate routes. The real version is less tidy. A new Associated Press report from aboard the European Union’s Operation Atalanta says movement through Hormuz remains severely disrupted, while the Bab el-Mandeb has become an alternate route for some Gulf oil exports. That same report quotes Atalanta’s commander saying there have been 79 pirate attacks in the region since Houthi attacks began in November 2023, with roughly 30 of them after the Iran war began in February 2026. That is not background noise. That is the backup route asking for hazard pay. AP reported the Atalanta figures on November 10, 2026.

This is the important distinction: a detour can solve one chokepoint and still create another bill. If ships avoid one dangerous gate only to move through another contested neighborhood, the market has not eliminated risk. It has repackaged it into slower voyages, tighter routing, armed watches, naval coordination, higher insurance scrutiny and more waiting. Very glamorous, in the way invoices are glamorous.

The bottom line: The backup route is not a free lane. If tankers, product carriers and escorts have to treat the Gulf of Aden like a live security zone, the cost shows up in fuel, freight and food before it shows up as a clean headline oil shock.

Why Seventy Nine Matters More Than Another War Headline

One attack can be dismissed as an incident. Seventy nine is a pattern. The number matters because piracy does not need to sink tankers to raise costs. It only has to make shipowners, charterers, insurers and crews behave as if a routine voyage is no longer routine.

For oil and refined products, that behavior has a price. Ships may slow down, alter course, sail in convoys, wait for guidance, hire additional security, or avoid certain windows. Every one of those choices burns time. Time is not abstract in shipping. It is vessel hire, crew cost, fuel consumption, port-slot uncertainty and cargo finance. When the cargo is diesel, jet fuel, gasoline blendstock, LPG or crude, those costs can land inside the delivered fuel price instead of appearing as a neat line item on your receipt.

That is why this is not just a naval-security story. It is a freight story. Notavello has already covered how the Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb turned from map labels into cost centers in the Bab el-Mandeb escort queue. Today’s development sharpens that point: the same corridor being asked to help relieve Hormuz pressure is also carrying a revived piracy threat.

Official Advisories Are Treating The Area Like A Live Risk Zone

The shipping industry is not relying on vibes here. The U.S. Maritime Administration has an active advisory for piracy, armed robbery and kidnapping for ransom covering the Gulf of Aden, Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean. The advisory tells U.S.-flagged commercial vessels to report attacks, suspicious activity and incidents immediately through naval and maritime-security channels. It is active through February 4, 2027. MARAD Advisory 2026-010 lays out the piracy and kidnapping risk.

There is also a separate MARAD advisory for the Red Sea, Bab el-Mandeb, Gulf of Aden, Arabian Sea and Somali Basin related to Houthi attacks on commercial vessels. That advisory says the Houthi maritime threat remains even though recent incidents have been fewer, and it points vessels toward Combined Maritime Forces guidance. MARAD Advisory 2026-013 covers the Houthi threat environment.

The practical lesson is simple: the route has stacked risks. Piracy, Houthi threats, naval blockades, tanker inspections and war-risk insurance do not politely wait in separate folders. A ship moving through the area has to plan for all of them at once. That raises the operating burden even on days when no vessel is hit.

The Fuel Market Is Already Too Tight To Laugh This Off

If diesel were cheap and inventories were fat, this would still matter, but it would matter less. They are not. The latest U.S. Energy Information Administration diesel update available before this post was published showed U.S. on-highway diesel at $6.292 a gallon for the week reported November 3, down six cents from the prior week but still $2.539 higher than a year earlier. EIA’s Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update publishes the weekly diesel price series.

That is why piracy risk in the Gulf of Aden can become a household issue without anyone at the grocery store ever saying “Bab el-Mandeb.” Diesel moves freight. It runs farm equipment. It powers construction machinery. It is baked into refrigerated trucking, package delivery, waste hauling and the boring middle miles of the economy. When the delivered cost of diesel stays high, the economy does not experience it as one dramatic oil-price chart. It experiences it as more expensive movement.

Crude prices get the television segment because crude has the cleaner ticker. Diesel gets the invoice. If shipping risk keeps refined-product flows slower or more expensive, the diesel market can stay uncomfortable even when crude briefly cools. That has been one of the uglier lessons of 2026: barrels are not the same thing as usable fuel in the right place at the right time.

This Is Not A Shortage Claim. It Is A Cost Claim.

There is a difference between saying “the world is out of fuel” and saying “moving fuel has become more expensive and fragile.” The first claim needs evidence of physical scarcity. The second needs evidence of disrupted routes, active security advisories, elevated fuel prices and ship operators changing behavior. Today’s evidence supports the second claim.

The Gulf of Aden piracy resurgence does not have to close the lane to matter. A few boarded vessels, suspected pirate action groups, naval warnings and insurance reviews are enough to change the commercial math. The market prices risk before the worst-case scenario arrives. That is irritating, but it is also how shipping survives. Nobody wants the bargain freight rate that comes with an unplanned hostage negotiation.

For ordinary people, the measurable consequence is not a pirate surcharge printed on a receipt. It is the persistence of high transport costs in the goods that move by truck, rail and ship. For farmers, it is diesel and fertilizer delivered through a stressed logistics chain. For refiners and fuel distributors, it is less flexibility when cargo timing gets weird. For governments, it is another reason emergency responses that focus only on crude supply can miss the refined-product bill.

The Real Signal Is Redundancy Getting Expensive

The energy system is supposed to have backups. Saudi Arabia has Red Sea export options. Tankers can reroute. Navies can escort. Buyers can swap suppliers. Traders can find replacement cargoes. All of that is true. It is also expensive.

The current signal from the Gulf of Aden is that redundancy itself is becoming a premium product. The market is not just paying for oil. It is paying for safe passage, schedule certainty, insurance comfort, naval attention and crew risk. Those are real inputs. They do not show up in the barrel count, which is why barrel-count analysis keeps underestimating the bill.

So the number to watch today is not only Brent, WTI or the latest tanker headline from Hormuz. It is 79. That figure says the alternate corridor has become part of the shock, not an escape from it. If the backup route needs escorts, advisories and a piracy watch, then the detour is no longer just a map solution. It is a freight bill with water on both sides.
