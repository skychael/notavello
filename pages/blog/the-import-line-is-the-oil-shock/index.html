The Import Line Is The Oil Shock

The latest U.S. oil report does not say America is out of fuel. It says the cushion is thinner, more expensive, and still tied to ships moving through a war zone.

The Crude Build Was The Least Comforting Part

The easy headline from this week’s oil data is that U.S. commercial crude inventories rose. The useful headline is that the rise did not fix the fuel problem.

For the week ending September 18, 2026, the U.S. Energy Information Administration reported commercial crude stocks at 426.4 million barrels, up 3.0 million barrels from the previous week. Fine. A build is a build. But the same table shows the Strategic Petroleum Reserve falling again, to 284.6 million barrels, while gasoline stocks dropped to 206.0 million barrels and distillate fuel oil stocks slipped to 107.4 million barrels. Those product tanks are what drivers, truckers, farmers, airlines and heating-oil customers actually feel. Crude in a tank is not diesel in a truck.

The EIA’s own weekly balance sheet also puts the price context in plain sight: regular gasoline averaged $4.478 per gallon on September 21, and on-highway diesel averaged $6.529. WTI at Cushing was $101.44 per barrel on September 18. You do not need a commodities terminal to understand that this is not a relaxed fuel market. You need a receipt. The data is in the EIA’s Weekly Petroleum Status Report table for the week ending September 18.

This is the part that makes the crude build a little dryly funny, in the way only energy statistics can be funny. Commercial crude rose, yes. Total stocks including the SPR were still 152.0 million barrels below the comparable week a year earlier. The pantry has one shelf that looks slightly better this week, while the basement reserve and the ready-to-use fuel shelves still look picked over.

The bottom line: The scary number is not just $6.529 diesel. It is the import line: the U.S. is leaning harder on crude inflows while gasoline and distillate stocks are below last year.

The Import Line Is Doing More Work

The cleaner signal is imports. The EIA reported crude oil net imports, including SPR movements, at a four-week average of 2.629 million barrels per day. That was up from 2.203 million barrels per day in the comparable year-ago period, a 19.4% increase. In plain English: the U.S. is leaning harder on foreign crude flows while the global shipping map is being redrawn by the Iran war.

That does not mean every extra barrel came through the Strait of Hormuz. Oil markets are not a cartoon map with one arrow and a villain at the end of it. But crude is priced globally, cargoes compete globally, and disruptions in the Persian Gulf change what refiners elsewhere will bid for replacement barrels. If Asian buyers need barrels from outside the Gulf, those barrels do not politely wait for U.S. drivers to finish filling up.

There is another awkward line in the same report: U.S. petroleum product exports averaged 7.824 million barrels per day over four weeks, up 14.4% from a year earlier. That is not a scandal by itself. The U.S. refining system is built into a global fuel market, and Gulf Coast refineries make products the world wants. But it does mean Americans are not shopping in a sealed domestic aisle. Diesel, gasoline components, jet fuel and other products are being pulled into a world market that is paying up for supply.

This is why the fuel bill can stay ugly even when someone points to a crude inventory build and says, see, the system is fine. The system is functioning. That is different from cheap.

Hormuz Risk Is Now A Working Cost, Not A Headline Risk

The timing matters. On September 21, the Associated Press reported that the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations Center said a tanker was struck by an unknown projectile as it headed into the Strait of Hormuz, injuring two crew members, while the vessel continued under its own power. AP also reported that some tankers were again using the strait, but traffic remained well below previous levels. That is not a full closure. It is also not normal trade with a nicer soundtrack.

You can read the AP report here: projectile strikes tanker passing through Strait of Hormuz.

The distinction matters because fuel shocks do not require a Hollywood blockade. A tanker can keep moving and still raise costs. A lane can stay technically open and still require more escorts, more waiting, more rerouting, more insurance, more caution from charterers, and more inventory held in the wrong place. The market does not wait for a formal announcement saying “shortage begins Monday.” It reprices when moving a barrel becomes slower, scarier, or less predictable.

That is also why the import line deserves attention. If the U.S. were sitting on bloated fuel stocks, a deep SPR cushion, and quiet shipping lanes, a single tanker incident might be market noise. Instead, it arrives while diesel is expensive, gasoline stocks are below last year, the SPR is far lower than a year ago, and crude import dependence is doing more work than it was doing last September.

Refineries Are Not A Magic Wand

Another number that should stop the victory lap: U.S. crude oil input to refineries fell by 519,000 barrels per day during the week, to 16.811 million barrels per day. The four-week average was still strong at 17.306 million barrels per day, 4.0% above the year-ago level, but weekly runs moved down at exactly the moment consumers want more finished fuel, not less.

That does not automatically mean refiners are failing. Refineries have maintenance, crude-quality constraints, regional logistics problems, and economic incentives. They are huge chemical plants, not microwaves. But when retail diesel is already above $6.50 and gasoline is flirting with $4.50, a half-million-barrel-per-day weekly drop in crude input is not decorative.

Distillate is the more serious kitchen-table number because it hides inside other bills. Diesel moves freight, powers farm equipment, supports construction, and competes with heating oil. The EIA showed distillate stocks at 107.4 million barrels, down 12.7% from a year earlier. That is the same pressure Notavello has been tracking in the diesel-gasoline gap: diesel does not just hit people who own diesel pickups. It shows up in freight rates, harvest costs, delivery fees and eventually grocery prices.

Gasoline is louder because the price is posted on signs the size of garage doors. Diesel is quieter because it is embedded in everything. Quiet does not mean small.

The SPR Is Buying Time, Not Ending The Problem

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell by 0.4 million barrels on the week, to 284.6 million barrels. That is 121.4 million barrels below the comparable week in 2025. The weekly draw is small compared with the big emergency releases people remember, but direction matters when the reserve is already much thinner than it was a year ago.

The SPR is useful because it can buy time. It cannot make tankers un-hit. It cannot turn sour crude into ultra-low-sulfur diesel at a refinery that is already constrained. It cannot force private inventories into the exact region where consumers need them. It also cannot be drawn forever without creating a second problem: the emergency cushion itself becomes the weak point.

There is a temptation to treat the SPR like a national coupon code. Prices high? Use the reserve. Election approaching? Use the reserve. Shipping risk ugly? Use the reserve. That logic works until the cupboard is no longer reassuring. A reserve is most powerful when the market believes it can be used decisively. A smaller reserve still helps, but it has less intimidation value.

This is why the current report feels uncomfortable. Commercial crude rose, but the emergency reserve fell. Fuel prices rose, and product stocks stayed weak. Imports are doing more work. Product exports are still large. The system is balancing, but it is balancing at a price ordinary people notice.

What To Watch Next

The next useful signals are not speeches. They are weekly numbers.

  • Crude net imports: If the four-week average keeps rising, the U.S. is leaning harder into global shipping risk.
  • Gasoline stocks: A crude build does not help drivers unless it turns into usable supply in the right markets.
  • Distillate stocks: This is the freight, farm and heating-oil warning light.
  • Refinery inputs: If runs fall during high-price weeks, product prices can stay sticky even if crude stops rising.
  • SPR direction: A continued draw means policy is still buying time instead of rebuilding the cushion.
  • Hormuz incident reports: Tankers do not need to sink to raise costs. Delays, escorts, insurance and routing choices are enough.

The strong version of today’s story is not “America is running out of oil.” It is more specific and more useful: America is paying more for fuel while relying on a global crude flow that is more exposed to war risk, and the domestic reserve cushion is not getting bigger.

That is the import line becoming the oil shock. It is boring enough to fit in a weekly table and expensive enough to land in your budget.
