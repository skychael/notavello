Hormuz Shuttle Tankers Made Freight The Oil Shock

The Strait of Hormuz is not behaving like a light switch. It is behaving like a toll road where the toll is being set by fear, waiting time and a very small pool of tanker owners willing to play.

The New Price Signal Is Not Just Brent

If you only watch Brent or WTI, you are missing the more annoying part of this oil shock. The market is not merely pricing barrels. It is pricing the right to move barrels through a war-shaped maze.

The concrete development this week is in tanker freight. Lloyd’s List reported that the Middle East Gulf-China TD3C VLCC index reached an unprecedented $759,969 per day on September 8, 2026, and that the rate was 26% above the March 16 peak during the earlier phase of the U.S.-Iran war. That is not a normal freight market having a spicy week. That is logistics turning into its own commodity shock. Lloyd’s List reported the VLCC rate surge and the tanker-market mechanics behind it.

The reason matters more than the number. The Strait of Hormuz is not fully closed, but the old pattern of simply loading a very large crude carrier inside the Gulf and sailing out is badly disrupted. More barrels are being pushed through a shuttle system: smaller or specially risk-tolerant movements across or near Hormuz, then ship-to-ship transfer in the Gulf of Oman, then onward long-haul movement on VLCCs. It is open, technically. It is also inefficient, slow and expensive. Fine print: that still ends up in the price of diesel, gasoline, jet fuel and petrochemical feedstocks. Funny how invoices travel better than ships.

The bottom line: Oil is moving, but it is moving badly. The new Hormuz workaround uses shuttle tankers and ship-to-ship transfers, and that means the freight bill is becoming part of the fuel price.

Ship-To-Ship Transfers Are Now The Workaround

The key phrase is ship-to-ship transfer, usually shortened to STS. Instead of a crude cargo taking the cleanest route from a Persian Gulf loading point to a buyer, part of the journey is being broken into pieces. Crude gets moved out through the danger zone, transferred offshore, and then loaded into long-haul tankers waiting outside the worst of the risk.

According to Lloyd’s List, Vortexa data showed crude exports via STS outside Hormuz rising from 603,000 barrels per day in April to 3.2 million barrels per day in June, 3.1 million barrels per day in July and 4.6 million barrels per day in August. Lloyd’s List also reported that the share of cross-Hormuz exports using STS recently surged to 80%. Those are the numbers that turn this from a maritime curiosity into a market story.

STS transfer is not magic. It adds handling, delay, coordination risk, weather risk, safety risk, legal review and insurance review. It ties up ships that would otherwise be doing simpler voyages. It creates waiting time in the Gulf of Oman. It pulls tanker supply toward the Middle East and away from other crude routes. Then West Africa, the U.S. Gulf and Brazil feel the squeeze too, because tanker supply is global. A ship waiting to receive cargo offshore Oman is not available to lift crude somewhere else.

That is why this is not just a Middle East story. The freight market is connected. Once the high-paying Hormuz shuttle trade starts dragging ships into the region, other refiners must pay more to secure vessels. The cost does not politely stay in the Strait.

Hormuz Was Too Big To Replace Cleanly

The reason the workaround is so ugly is simple: Hormuz was enormous before the war. The U.S. Energy Information Administration said flows through the Strait of Hormuz in 2024 and the first quarter of 2025 represented more than one-quarter of global seaborne oil trade and about one-fifth of global petroleum liquids consumption. The EIA also said around one-fifth of global LNG trade moved through Hormuz in 2024, primarily from Qatar. The EIA’s Hormuz chokepoint analysis lays out the scale of the oil and LNG exposure.

That is why “use another route” is an answer only if you are trying to win an argument on television. It is not a complete operating plan. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates do have pipelines that can bypass the strait, but the EIA estimated before the latest escalation that only about 2.6 million barrels per day of capacity from Saudi and UAE pipelines could be available to reroute additional volumes in a disruption. Hormuz normally handled many times that amount.

The squeeze became harsher after Saudi Arabia closed its East-West pipeline following a drone attack. The Associated Press reported on September 14, 2026, that repairs could take three to five weeks, citing regional officials, and that Rystad Energy estimated 2.6 million to 4 million barrels per day had been moving through the pipeline and out of Yanbu since late August. AP’s report on the East-West pipeline closure explains why the backup route is now part of the problem.

That does not mean every lost pipeline barrel vanishes forever. Inventories, rerouting and partial Hormuz traffic can soften the hit. But the replacement system is not a clean swap. It is a patchwork, and patchworks have seams. Seams are where costs leak through.

Why This Reaches Your Fuel Bill

The ordinary-person consequence is not that you pay a line item called “Gulf of Oman ship-to-ship transfer surcharge” at the pump. That would at least be honest, which is why it will never happen. The consequence is that refiners and traders pay more for crude delivered on time, more for freight, more for insurance and more for optionality. Then refined products clear at higher prices when supply is tight.

S&P Global reported that Platts Dated Brent rallied to $114.26 per barrel on September 9, up from $89.64 per barrel on August 28, as tanker strikes and Middle East supply disruption widened physical crude premiums and pushed tanker freight higher. The same report said the Arab Gulf-Far East VLCC rate reached an all-time high of $161.93 per metric ton on September 9. S&P Global’s market report connects the crude-price move, freight rates and physical premiums.

Diesel is the product to watch because it is the working fuel of the economy. Trucks burn it. Farm equipment burns it. Railroads use it. Construction equipment uses it. Backup generators use it when the grid is having a day. When crude logistics get more expensive and refineries must bid harder for suitable barrels, diesel does not need a dramatic shortage headline to become more painful. It only needs enough friction to lift replacement costs.

This is the same basic chain that shows up in freight-heavy consumer prices: fuel moves crops, groceries, packages, building materials and imported goods. A higher tanker bill is not the whole inflation story. It is one more brick in the wall. For a related U.S. fuel-market angle, Notavello has covered how diesel exports can keep the fuel bill hot even when headline crude numbers look less terrifying.

Open Is Not The Same As Normal

One trap in this story is treating Hormuz as binary. Closed means crisis; open means relief. That is too neat, and energy markets are rarely neat unless someone is trying to sell you a chart.

AP reported that Lloyd’s List Intelligence counted 90 Strait of Hormuz transits in the first week of September, compared with roughly 130 ships per day before the war. That does not describe a reopened artery. It describes a constrained channel where only some operators, cargoes and routes make economic or security sense. The difference between those two conditions is where freight inflation lives.

There was another reminder on September 15. The U.S. military said it destroyed two small Iranian boats that were trying to take a U.S. Navy sea drone near the Strait of Hormuz, according to AP. The hardware is not the point for this post. The point is that the same corridor now requires military protection, special routing, insurer approval, cautious owners and workaround logistics. AP’s report on the sea-drone incident shows the security pressure still surrounding the corridor.

Markets can live with risk. They just charge for it. The charge shows up first in war-risk premiums, freight rates, demurrage, ship availability and crude differentials. Then it moves downstream into refinery economics and fuel prices. By the time consumers notice, the original cause has been laundered through six boring invoices. Boring invoices are undefeated.

What To Watch Next

The useful question now is not whether someone announces that Hormuz is open. The useful question is whether the expensive workaround starts shrinking. Watch three things.

  • STS volumes outside Hormuz: If ship-to-ship transfers stay near August levels, the market is still paying for a broken route even if some vessels are moving.
  • VLCC day rates and Gulf of Oman loading rates: Falling crude prices will not mean much for delivered fuel costs if freight remains extreme.
  • Saudi pipeline repair timing: If the East-West pipeline returns closer to the three-week end of the repair window, the market gets some relief. If it drifts toward five weeks or longer, the tanker workaround has to carry more weight.

The uncomfortable lesson is that the oil shock has moved from the dramatic part of the map to the operational part of the spreadsheet. Hormuz does not have to be sealed shut to hurt. It only has to be unreliable enough that every barrel needs extra ships, extra time and extra nerve.
