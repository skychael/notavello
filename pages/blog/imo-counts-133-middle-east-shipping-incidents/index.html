IMO Counts 133 Middle East Shipping Incidents As Tanker Attacks Continue

The Strait of Hormuz is not closed. That is the comforting part, and also the misleading part.

IMO Put A Number On The Shipping War

The concrete development today is not a speech, a rumor, or another map with arrows on it. The International Maritime Organization now lists 133 confirmed incidents in the Strait of Hormuz and the wider Middle East as of November 10, 2026, with 25 confirmed seafarer fatalities. That is the kind of number insurers, charterers, refiners and grain shippers cannot politely file under background noise.

The latest entries are doing the work. IMO lists the NAVIG8 MACALLISTER and HUDA as damaged on November 7 near Oman, VIEILA damaged on November 5 east of Khasab, VELOS SAPPHIRE damaged on November 4 north of Qatar with one seafarer fatality, and GRIT damaged on November 3 northeast of Lima, Oman. This is not a single spectacular closure of a chokepoint. It is a grinding security tax on ships that still have to move.

On November 10, United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations issued another warning after a tanker master reported being struck by an unknown projectile 19 nautical miles northeast of Lima, Oman. UKMTO said the crew were safe and no environmental impact had been reported, with damage assessment to follow at first light. That last sentence is doing a lot of work. Safe crew and no spill is the good outcome. A tanker hit close enough to the Hormuz entrance to require daylight inspection is still a serious market signal.

The bottom line: IMO now lists 133 confirmed Middle East shipping incidents and 25 seafarer fatalities. U.S. diesel is still $6.127 a gallon. That is how a shipping war becomes a grocery, farm and freight problem without a formal blockade.

This Is Route Risk, Not Just Oil Price Risk

The lazy version of this story asks whether Hormuz is open or closed. The useful version asks how expensive and slow it is becoming to use. Ships can still move through the Gulf, but every projectile, mine report, escort request and damage inspection changes the calculation for owners who have to decide whether a voyage is worth it.

ADNOC Logistics and Services said one of its vessels was damaged in an attack in UAE territorial waters on the afternoon of November 10, according to the Emirates News Agency. The company did not identify the ship or the extent of the damage. That uncertainty is exactly the point. Markets do not need perfect information to raise risk premiums. They only need enough repeat incidents to believe the next cargo may also need escorts, delays, rerouting, repair surveys or higher insurance.

Reuters reported on November 11 that Brent crude rose to $92.53 a barrel and WTI to $86.71 as attacks on shipping offset hopes for de-escalation in the Iran war. The same report cited data showing 37 ships attacked in October, the highest monthly total since the start of the conflict. When price relief depends on diplomacy but freight risk depends on whether tankers are still getting hit, you do not have one oil market. You have two arguments happening at the same time, naturally in the most expensive way possible.

This is why the practical framing is closer to Hormuz as a shipping-lane screening problem than Hormuz as a single dramatic blockade story. A chokepoint does not have to shut to matter. It only has to become unreliable enough that ordinary movement needs extra money, time and armed patience.

Diesel Is Still The Number Households Feel

The U.S. retail fuel data gives this story its domestic edge. EIA’s latest gasoline and diesel update shows the national average on-highway diesel price at $6.127 a gallon on November 9. Yes, that was down 16.5 cents from the previous week. No, that does not make it cheap. It was still $2.29 a gallon above the same week a year earlier.

The regional numbers are worse where freight already hurts. EIA put West Coast diesel at $7.110 a gallon and California diesel at $7.897. The Midwest, which matters for farms, processors and inland freight, was still at $6.201. New England was at $6.349 and the Central Atlantic at $6.412, which is not exactly what heating-oil households want to see in November.

Gasoline also matters, but diesel is the pass-through machine. It runs trucks, harvest equipment, construction fleets, refrigerated trailers, rail-linked drayage and a large share of the invisible logistics that sit behind a grocery shelf. When diesel stays above $6, it does not politely remain a line item for trucking companies. It walks into food, building materials, delivery fees, municipal budgets and small-business margins.

That is the measurable consequence for ordinary people. You may not buy crude oil. You may not know what a VLCC is. You still buy things moved by diesel. Very democratic, in the worst possible sense.

EIA Just Raised The 2027 Diesel Outlook

The more important EIA detail is not only today’s pump number. It is the forecast change. In its November Short-Term Energy Outlook, EIA raised its projected average U.S. retail diesel price to $5.25 a gallon for 2026 and $5.03 for 2027. The previous forecast was $5.19 for 2026 and $4.49 for 2027, meaning the 2027 diesel forecast jumped 12%.

That is a quiet but nasty revision. It says the agency does not expect diesel pain to vanish when one week’s crude price falls or one diplomatic headline sounds less terrible. It expects elevated fuel costs to linger into next year. For fleets, farmers and contractors, that changes bids. For retailers, it changes distribution math. For households, it means fewer clean breaks between an oil shock and the checkout line.

The reason is not mysterious. The crude barrel is only the first input. Diesel also depends on refinery capacity, distillate inventories, global middle-distillate demand, import availability, freight, insurance, and the regional distribution system. Add repeated tanker attacks near the world’s most watched oil chokepoint and the discount fairy does not arrive on schedule.

There is a temptation to cheer every pullback in crude as the end of the problem. That is fine for a headline. It is less useful if you run a trucking route, buy fertilizer, heat with oil, or price food distribution six months out.

Do Not Invent An Inventory Story Before The Report Lands

One important caution: the official U.S. weekly petroleum inventory report is not out today. EIA says the Weekly Petroleum Status Report will be released on Thursday, November 12, because the federal government is closed on Wednesday, November 11. So today is not the day to pretend we know the official weekly change in U.S. distillate stocks, crude stocks, refinery runs or product supplied.

Reuters reported that market sources citing American Petroleum Institute data showed U.S. crude stocks rose by 9 million barrels in the week ended November 6. That may shape trading, but API data is not the same as the official EIA report. For diesel and heating-oil risk, the distillate line matters more than the crude build anyway. A crude inventory increase can coexist with tight diesel, weak refinery flexibility or expensive regional supply. Markets are annoying like that because physics keeps showing up.

The disciplined read is simple: wait for EIA’s official November 12 tables before making a hard inventory claim. Today’s verified facts are already enough. Tankers are still being hit. IMO’s incident count is high. Diesel remains above $6 nationally. EIA’s 2027 diesel forecast moved sharply higher. That is a story without needing to decorate it.

What To Watch Next

If you want to track whether this becomes better or worse for real-world bills, watch four things.

  • UKMTO and IMO incident updates: one-off attacks are bad; clustering near Oman, Khasab, Qatar, UAE waters or the Hormuz lanes is worse.
  • EIA distillate inventories: diesel and heating oil are the household and freight pressure points, not just crude.
  • Regional diesel spreads: California, the West Coast, New England and the Central Atlantic show where logistics pain is already concentrated.
  • Freight and insurance behavior: if owners demand more money to enter the Gulf, the oil price is only part of the bill.

The uncomfortable lesson is that a partially functioning Strait of Hormuz can still be expensive enough to matter. No grand closure announcement is required. A few more damaged ships, a few more cautious owners, and a few more weeks of $6 diesel can do plenty of work on the economy all by themselves.
